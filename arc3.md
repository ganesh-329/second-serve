# Second Serve: Arc 3 chapters (Tokyo expedition and summer camp)

## Chapter 30: Red pen

Tanaka was already on the step when Sho came up the hill on Monday, sitting on it with the key on its red string hanging from 1 finger and his chin on his fist, watching the bottom of the path.

When Sho came round the corner by the shrine Tanaka stood up and put the key in the lock and turned it. *Clack.*

It was 4:56. The air was already warm, the 1st properly warm morning of the summer, and the sky over the school had gone pale and clean, with 1 long thin cloud lying over the hills behind the gym.

"Early," said Sho.

"EARLIER THAN YOU," said Tanaka, and pushed the door open with his whole arm.

They got the net out of the storage room. It was a 2-person job, and for a week the net had stayed rolled up on its pole in the corner, because Tanaka had been on his own. Tanaka took the left post and Sho took the right, and they walked it out across the floor between them, the net sagging in the middle like a hammock. The left post's crank stuck halfway, the way it always did, and Tanaka hit it with the heel of his hand and it went. Sho held the post steady while Tanaka cranked. The cable came up tight. Tanaka slapped the tape with his flat hand, and it hummed.

"Light," said Tanaka. "Coach said light."

"Light."

They passed. Back and forth across the net, slow, the ball going up and down between them in the blue light from the high windows. Tanaka in his FIGHTING SPIRIT shirt with the backward R, yelling "NICE!" when Sho passed it to him and "MINE!" when it came back, as if there were anybody else in the gym it could belong to.

After a while Tanaka started hitting it at him instead, slow down-balls, 1 a little to Sho's left, then a little to his right, then 1 short, dropping in front of him. Sho got his feet to all of them and his arms under all of them, and the ball went up, 1 after another, and came down in Tanaka's hands on the other side of the net.

At 5:40 Tanaka caught the ball and stood holding it.

"Your eyes."

"Fine."

"Fine fine? Or fine like you said with the nose?"

"Fine fine."

Tanaka gave him the intimidation face, which on Tanaka also meant he was thinking. Then he nodded once, hard, threw the ball up, and spiked it at the floor by Sho's feet, a little harder than before. Sho was already there. It went straight up off his arms, high and clean, and came down into Tanaka's hands.

"Okay," said Tanaka. "Good."

---

At the start of practice that afternoon, before warm-up, Ukai stood in front of the club with his clipboard.

"Monday," he said. "Exams. Who's in trouble."

Nobody moved.

"I said Monday. It's Monday. Who's in trouble."

Slowly, from the middle of the half circle, Hinata's hand went up. A beat later, from a different part of the half circle, Kageyama's hand went up.

Then Noya's. Then, after a long silence, Tanaka's, at shoulder height, as if he were hoping it wouldn't count if it didn't go all the way.

"Which subjects," said Ukai.

"All of them," said Hinata.

"English," said Kageyama. "And the rest."

"English," said Noya. "And math. And Classical. And maybe history."

Ukai looked at Tanaka.

"Classical," said Tanaka, to the floor. "I got 33 last time."

Down the half circle, somebody made a small sound. Tanaka's head came round toward the back of the room, toward Sho, with his eyes narrowed. Sho kept his face exactly where it was.

"33's a pass," said Ukai. "Barely. Who are you studying with."

"Noya. At my place." Tanaka scratched his head. "My sister says she'll make us. She says if we don't pass she'll drive us to Tokyo herself, and we'll have to sit in the back with the dog."

"Fine. And you 2." Ukai looked at Hinata and Kageyama, a few places apart, both staring at the floor. "Who are you studying with?"

Hinata opened his mouth. Kageyama opened his. They both shut them again.

"Sort it out," said Ukai. "By tomorrow." He turned a page on his clipboard. "Warm up."

---

The receive drill was the one they did every day. Ukai on a chair on the far side of the net with a cart of balls, serving floats. The receivers coming through in a line, Daichi, Noya, Asahi, Tanaka, Ennoshita, Narita, Kinoshita, round and round, and the pass going up to Suga at the net, who caught it and dropped it in a basket.

"Kiyose." Ukai pointed at the floor beside the end line, behind the receivers. "There. You call. Who passed, and if it's good. Every 3rd turn you take 1 yourself, light."

"Yes."

Ukai served. Daichi passed.

"Daichi-san. Good."

Noya. "Noya-san. Good."

Asahi. "Asahi-san. A bit high."

Tanaka. The ball came off his arms, and Sho heard it go wrong before he saw it, the flat hard note it made when it hit the bone, and his eyes went to Tanaka's left arm, which was lower than his right, and then to the ball, which was going short.

"Tanaka-senpai. Left. Short."

Suga had to take a step in for it.

"I KNOW," said Tanaka.

"Keep calling," said Ukai, from the chair.

Ennoshita. "Good." Narita. The sound came, stiff and straight-armed, the same sound Asahi made, and Sho's eyes went to the arms that had made it, and it was Narita. "Narita-san. Good." Kinoshita. "Kinoshita-san. Short."

The line went round again, and again. Somewhere in the 3rd time round Sho noticed that Noya, after each of his own passes, was turning his head a fraction toward the back of the court. Waiting for the call. When it came, "Noya-san, good," he punched the air, small, by his hip.

Daichi kept his head still. After Sho said "Daichi-san, a bit fast," Daichi's next pass came up off his arms softer, with more air under it, and dropped into Suga's hands like something being set down on a table.

Tanaka, after his 3rd "Left," stood in the line and lifted his left arm and looked at it as if it belonged to someone else. When his turn came round again he bent lower, and the ball came off both arms evenly, and Sho said "Tanaka-senpai, good," and Tanaka yelled "HA!" so loud that Ukai's next serve went into the net.

"Oi."

"Sorry, Coach!"

Every 3rd turn Sho went to the front of the line and took a pass himself.

The 1st was easy, a float straight at him, and he passed it up to Suga, a little toward the antenna. The 2nd was the same. Then, on his 3rd turn, Ukai looked at him over the cart of balls a beat longer than usual before he tossed.

Sho watched him.

Ukai's toss went up, a little further to his right than the others had, and his shoulder opened as he swung, turning out toward the sideline. His hand came through the ball flat, and Sho's feet were already going, 1 step back and to the right, before the ball had crossed the net. The ball came over flat and fast toward the seam between him and Noya, dipping in the last 3 meters, and he was there, and it came into his arms, and he let it go up, high, soft, in the middle of the court.

Suga caught it without moving his feet.

"Nice," said Suga.

On the chair, Ukai had the next ball in his hand and wasn't tossing it.

"You went before it crossed," said Ukai.

"His shoulder opened, Coach. Yours. You turned it out."

Ukai looked at his own shoulder, as if it had told on him.

"Hm," he said. He tossed the next ball. "Again."

Sho went to the back of the line. At the end of the drill Ukai got down off the chair and came round the net with his clipboard. He'd been keeping a column of little ticks the whole time, Sho saw, between serves.

"How many'd you call wrong?"

"3. Maybe 4."

"I had you at 4." Ukai tucked the clipboard under his arm. "Tomorrow, same. Ears and eyes. You hear it, you look where it tells you."

He walked away across the court, and at the far end Sho heard him say, not loudly, to Takeda, who'd come in during the drill with a stack of papers, "Kid reads servers like a book," and Takeda say, "Is that good?" and Ukai say, "It's IQ. You can't teach it," and blow his whistle.

---

On Tuesday at lunch Sho went down to the 1st-year corridor to buy bread and stopped by the noticeboard halfway along it, because at the far end, by the window outside class 1-4, Tsukishima was leaning on the sill with his headphones round his neck and a carton of milk tea, and Yamaguchi was beside him eating an onigiri, and Hinata had just come up the stairs at the other end of the corridor at a run.

Hinata saw Tsukishima. He slowed down.

From the vending machines in the alcove halfway along, Kageyama came out with a carton of milk. He saw Tsukishima too, and stopped.

He saw Hinata. Hinata saw him.

Both of them turned and looked at Tsukishima.

Then they both started walking toward the window, Hinata from the stairs and Kageyama from the alcove, at almost exactly the same speed, each with his eyes fixed on Tsukishima and nowhere else.

Sho read the poster on the noticeboard. It was about the swimming pool.

Hinata got there 1 step ahead. He stopped in front of Tsukishima and bowed, deep, the whole way down. Kageyama arrived a step later, a meter away from Hinata, and stood there with the carton of milk in his hand and his mouth slightly open, and didn't bow.

"Please teach us!" said Hinata, to the floor. "For exams! Please!"

Tsukishima looked down at the top of Hinata's head. Then at Kageyama, who was still standing up straight.

"And the King?" said Tsukishima. "Is he asking too, or is he just here for the view?"

Kageyama's face went through several things. His jaw worked. The carton in his hand made a small crumpling noise.

"...Please," said Kageyama. It came out of him like a cork coming out of a bottle. Then he bowed too, abruptly, from the waist, a little too far, so that the milk carton nearly hit the floor. "Teach us. English. And the rest."

Yamaguchi had put his onigiri down on the windowsill. His shoulders were shaking.

"Tsukki," he said, not quite steadily. "They're asking nicely."

Tsukishima drank some of his milk tea. He looked out of the window at the field for a while, at the baseball team's net sagging in the heat.

"Library," he said at last. "After practice. Every day till exams." He looked down at the 2 bowed heads. "And if you 2 start fighting in the library, I'm leaving, and you can fail on your own."

"Yes!" said Hinata.

"...Yes," said Kageyama.

They came up out of the bow together, and both noticed, and both looked away from each other, and walked off in opposite directions, Hinata toward the stairs and Kageyama back to the vending machines, where he stood for a long time staring at the buttons as if he'd forgotten what milk was.

Sho went on down the corridor to buy his bread. As he passed the window Tsukishima's eyes moved to him, briefly, over the top of the milk tea, and moved away again.

---

On Wednesday after practice the library was full.

It was on the 3rd floor at the end of the corridor, and in exam season it was the most crowded room in the school. Every table was taken. The windows were open because the radiators were off and the heat had come in early, and outside in the cherry trees the 1st cicadas were starting, a dry rattling sound that came and went.

Suga had the last table by the window. He'd put his bag on the chair opposite to keep it, and moved it when Sho came in.

At a table on the far side of the room, under the clock, Tsukishima sat with a red pen in his hand and 2 practice tests in front of him, 1 from Hinata and 1 from Kageyama, which Hinata and Kageyama had pushed across to him from opposite ends of the table. Tsukishima was marking them. The red pen went down each page, and each time it moved Hinata flinched, and Kageyama didn't, and Yamaguchi, sitting next to Tsukishima, watched the pen and winced on their behalf.

"Center Exam," said Suga, tapping the thick blue book in front of him. "English. This one's going to kill me." He tapped the other book, older and thinner, with a paper cover gone soft. "And this one's yours."

*Classical Japanese Grammar: Complete Review.*

"I got 72 in Classical last year," said Suga. "So. English for Classical."

Sho sat down.

They did English 1st, because Suga had a practice test the next morning and said he was about 3 grammar points away from crying. Sho looked at the paper. Long reading passages, the kind he'd read a thousand of in the other life: scouting reports from Italy and Poland and Brazil run through bad translation software, which he'd then had to rewrite by hand into 3 lines a coach could read before a match. The grammar in Suga's book was ordinary. The trick, he'd learned in a room with the lights off, had never been the grammar.

"Don't read all of it straight off," said Sho. "Find the sentence that's doing the work."

"How do you find it?"

"It's usually the 1st or the last. Or the one with *however* in it. The rest is getting there." He put his finger on a paragraph about recycling in Sweden. "This one. *However, the program has struggled to...* That's the paragraph."

Suga read the sentence twice. He looked at the question under it, circled an answer, and turned to the answer key at the back.

"Huh," he said.

"Do the next one."

Suga did the next one. He read the paragraph through once, quickly, with his pencil moving down the margin, and stopped at a sentence near the end and read that one again, slowly, and circled his answer. Right. The one after that, he read and stopped and frowned, and read the 1st sentence again, and the last, and chose, and got it wrong, and swore at the book under his breath in the polite way he swore at things, and the 1st year at the next table looked round.

"*Although*," said Sho, pointing. "Not *however*. Same job."

Suga wrote it in the margin. *Although = however.* He did 6 more. He got 5.

"I watch a lot of movies," said Sho, before Suga could ask.

"Hm," said Suga, and slid the paper-covered book across the table.

Classical was worse.

Suga explained it with his hands. The verb endings that changed if the sentence was a question. The particle that meant 2 different things depending on what came after it. The way the subject vanished from sentences for whole paragraphs, and you were meant to know, from the honorifics on the verbs, whether it was the emperor or a lady-in-waiting doing the crying.

"This ending," said Suga, tapping it with his pencil, "means somebody important. So it's the emperor. It tells you who. You just have to hear it."

Sho looked at the sentence. *Ima wa mukashi...* He read it again under his breath, the endings, the shape of the sound of them.

"...The emperor's crying."

"The emperor's crying." Suga sat back. "Yes."

They went on. The light outside went from white to gold. Across the room Tsukishima finished marking and pushed the 2 tests back across the table, 1 to each end, and Hinata looked at his and put his forehead down on the table, and Kageyama looked at his and went very still, and Yamaguchi said "Tsukki, that's a lot of red," and Tsukishima said "That's their red. I just found it."

At 6:30, when the librarian started turning off the lights at the far end of the room, Suga took a sheet of paper out of the Classical book. It was a practice test, 1 page, photocopied, the kind the teachers handed out in exam week.

"Do this one," said Suga. "15 minutes. I'll mark it."

Sho did it. He did it the way he'd been doing the sentences all evening, out loud under his breath, listening to the endings. Some of them he heard. Some of them, halfway through a sentence, the subject slid away from him, and he couldn't tell whether it was the emperor or the lady or a monk who'd come in 2 lines ago, and he guessed.

At 15 minutes Suga held out his hand, and Sho gave him the paper.

Suga took a red pen out of his pencil case. He marked it the way Tsukishima had marked Hinata's, steadily, page down, the pen moving and stopping and moving. A tick. A tick. A line through a whole answer. Another line. A tick. A small circle round a verb ending, with a red arrow and a word next to it.

At the bottom of the page he wrote a number and put the pen down and turned the paper round.

*38.*

In red, circled.

Sho looked at it.

"It's 3 lower than last time," said Suga. "It's not a bad paper. This one's harder than the last test. But." He tapped the number, gently, with the back of the pen. "The line's 30. That's 8 away."

8 away. 8 points, out of 100, between him and a make-up test on a Saturday morning in Miyagi, while a bus went south to Tokyo without him.

He thought about the Nekoma page at the back of his notebook. *What does he see now?* He thought about a gym he'd never been in, and Kenma in it with no expression at all, waiting, holding the ball 1 beat too long for somebody who wasn't going to be there to look.

Across the room, Hinata had lifted his forehead off the table and was looking at his own red marks, and then, very slowly, picking up his pencil.

"Same time tomorrow," said Sho.

Suga raised an eyebrow. "It's Wednesdays."

"Tomorrow too," said Sho. "Please. And Friday. I'll do your English every day."

Suga looked at him, and at the red 38, and at him again.

"You really want Tokyo," said Suga.

"Yes," said Sho.

Suga capped the red pen.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Same table. Bring the emperor."

## Chapter 31: Villager B

On Thursday at lunch Kiyoko came down the 1st-year corridor, looking into the classrooms.

Sho was coming back up from the school store with a melon bread and a carton of milk, and he saw her as he came off the stairs. She came from the far end, from 1-1, slowly, with her bag on her shoulder and the light from the windows catching her glasses, and at each classroom door she paused and looked in, and moved on.

The corridor went quiet around her as she came. 3rd years didn't come to the 1st-year floor. Shimizu Kiyoko of the volleyball club was known by sight to every 1st year in the school, and none of them had ever expected her to look at them.

1-2. 1-3. At 1-4 Tsukishima, at the window with his milk tea, raised his eyebrows a fraction and lowered them again. Yamaguchi stood up straighter.

At 1-5 Kiyoko stopped. She looked past the door, down the corridor toward Sho, at 3 girls coming back from the vending machines with juice boxes. The 2 on the outside were talking. The 1 in the middle was listening, and laughing a beat after the others: a small girl with light hair tied up on 1 side, holding her juice box in both hands.

Yachi Hitoka. She sat in the front row of 1-5, by the door, and wrote everything down in small, neat handwriting, and had the top mark in the class on the last math test, and jumped when people said her name. In May she'd dropped her pencil case in this corridor and Sho had picked up 3 of the pens for her, and she'd said thank you so many times he'd started to wonder what he'd done.

Kiyoko stepped into the girls' path.

The 2 on the outside stopped. Yachi walked another half step before she noticed, and then stopped so suddenly the juice box squelched in her hands.

"Excuse me," said Kiyoko. "Are you a 1st year?"

"Y-yes?"

"Are you in a club?"

"N-no. Not yet. I mean, no. I'm not."

"Would you like to be a manager? For the boys' volleyball club."

Yachi looked up at her with her mouth a little open and the juice box forgotten. Her 2 friends had each taken a small step backward.

"Yes," said Yachi.

A beat later her face caught up with what she'd said.

"I mean... That is... What does a manager...?"

"Come and see," said Kiyoko. "After school. The Second Gymnasium." She inclined her head, very slightly. "Thank you."

She walked back up the corridor the way she'd come. When she'd turned the corner by the stairs the noise came back all at once, and Yachi's friends grabbed her by both arms.

"Hitoka! That was *Shimizu-senpai*!"

"I said yes." Yachi's voice had gone thin and high. "Why did I say yes? I don't know anything about volleyball. I don't know what a manager *does*. What if they make me carry things? What if I drop them? What if I drop something important and it breaks and they lose and they make me pay for it and I have to sell my..."

Her friends steered her into the classroom. On the way past, her eyes landed on Sho, standing aside in the doorway with his bread and his milk, and stopped.

"Kiyose-kun. You're in the volleyball club."

"Yes."

"Are they..." She held the juice box to her chest. "Are they scary?"

Sho thought about it.

"They're loud," he said. "Tanaka-senpai makes faces. He means them nicely. Hinata shouts about everything. Noya-senpai is short and shouts louder than Hinata." He thought some more. "Kiyoko-san's the quietest one there. When she says something, everybody stops."

Yachi looked at him for a while.

Then she bowed, quickly, a small bob from the waist, and let her friends pull her to her desk.

---

She came to the gym after school with Kiyoko, holding the strap of her school bag in both hands, and sat on the edge of the stage where Kiyoko pointed.

Sho saw her come in from the end line. He was where Ukai had put him on Monday, behind the receive line, calling the passes and taking every 3rd 1 himself.

"Noya-san. Good. Tanaka-senpai. Good."

On the stage Yachi jumped every time Tanaka yelled. Her eyes went from the receive drill to the far court, and stayed there.

On the far court, Hinata and Kageyama had a cart of balls between them.

They still weren't speaking. Ukai had put them on the far net together anyway, and Kageyama tossed and Hinata ran, and neither of them said a word. The 1st 2 went the old way. Hinata in the air before the ball left Kageyama's hands, his eyes shut, the ball arriving at his palm as if it had always been going there, the crack of it on the floor. On the stage Yachi leaned forward with her mouth open.

On the 3rd, Hinata kept his eyes open.

Sho saw it from the far end of the gym, because he'd been waiting for it. Hinata's eyes stayed wide all the way up, on the ball, and he was a fraction late, the quick already past the place where his hand arrived. He swung at the air. The ball went off the end of his fingers and over the antenna. He landed and stood looking at his own palm.

Kageyama took the next ball out of the cart without a word, and tossed it, the old way, fast. Hinata looked at it go past him. Then he ran for the next one, eyes shut, and it landed.

Yachi flinched at the crack and leaned forward again. To her it had been 2 of the most beautiful things she'd ever seen and a 3rd that went over the antenna, and she didn't know the difference yet.

Sho's turn came round. He went to the front of the line.

Ukai, on his chair on the far side of the net, picked a ball out of the cart and held it.

Before the toss Sho looked once at the other side of the net, all of it at once. Ukai's chair. The cart. Suga at the net with the basket by his feet. Ennoshita at the side, picking up a stray ball. The empty court where the blockers would be. He took it in 1 look and let it go, and then he was looking only at Ukai: the ball in his left hand, the right arm drawn back.

The toss went up, a little left of the last one. Ukai's chest stayed square. The arm came through straight.

*Straight down the middle. At me.*

Sho didn't move his feet. He let his weight sink and kept his arms together and still, and the ball came over the net flat and dipping, and it came to him, exactly where he was standing, and went up off his arms in a soft high curve into Suga's hands.

"Nice," said Suga.

"Daichi-san," said Sho, going back to the end line, because Daichi was already passing the next one. "Good."

At the water break Ennoshita came over with the basket of loose balls.

"Kiyose."

"Yes."

"You go still," said Ennoshita. "Right before. Every time."

Sho held the ball he'd been about to put in the basket.

"Someone said that once," he said.

"Thought so." Ennoshita took the ball out of his hand and put it in the basket. "I watch a lot, from the line."

He carried the basket off toward the cart.

Sho stood at the end of the court.

Oikawa had said it at the sink, after Seijoh. *You'd go still and look at them, and you'd already be moving.* But that had been the day the eye fired, the day of the strange one, with Oikawa's serve coming at him and his whole head full of light.

Today the eye hadn't fired. Today it had been an ordinary drill, an ordinary float, and he'd gone still anyway, every time, enough for Ennoshita to see it from the side of the court.

It was him now. His body had learned to stop and wait for the read, and it did it whether the eye came or not, and anybody who watched him for long enough could see it.

He went to the stage, to his bag at the far end from Yachi, and got the notebook out, and turned to the back, under the Nekoma box, under *What does he see now?*

*I go still before every read. With the eye or without. Every time. Ennoshita saw it from the line.*

And under that:

*So Kenma will.*

---

He did Classical with Suga every day after that, in the library, at the window table, the way he'd asked.

On Thursday it was 44. On Friday it was 47. Suga marked them in red and pushed them back across the table, and they did English for Suga after, every day, the trade going both ways, and Suga's practice scores went 8 out of 10, then 9.

On Friday, at lunch, Hinata came running down the 1st-year corridor shouting "YACHI-SAN!" at the top of his voice.

Yachi went out into the corridor white as paper, and the 2 of them stood by the window outside 1-5 for most of lunch: Hinata talking with both hands, Yachi listening with both hands clutched to her chest. Sho was at his desk at the back by the window, with his Classical book open and his bread, and Hinata came through the open door the way he came through most walls. ## Chapter 32: Flying falls

The bus was at the school gate at 5:40, white, with *Karasuno High School* on a card in the windscreen in Takeda's handwriting. The engine was running. The driver was a man of about 60 in a cap, reading a sports newspaper with his feet up on the dashboard.

It was barely light. Sho had his bag on 1 shoulder and the bundle Mom had tied for him in the other hand: onigiri, 4 of them, and a frozen bottle of barley tea wrapped in a towel to keep them cold. In the inside pocket of his bag, zipped shut, the permission form in its clear plastic folder, and next to it, folded, a Classical test with *58* on it in red.

Everybody was there except 2.

"They'll be fine," said Tanaka, to the street. "They'll do the test, and they'll pass it, and Saeko-nee will bring them."

"Saeko-nee drives like a demon," said Noya.

"She drives like a demon," Tanaka agreed. "They'll be fine."

Ukai came out of the convenience store across the road with a coffee and a bag of bread rolls and got on without counting anybody. Kiyoko sat at the front with the first-aid box on her knees. Yachi sat beside her with a clipboard she'd been given on Monday and hadn't put down since, reading the schedule for the 3rd time with her lips moving.

2 seats near the front were empty. Nobody sat in them. Nobody put their bags on them.

It was nearly 5 hours to Tokyo.

Most of the bus slept for the 1st 2. Tanaka slept with his mouth open and his head on Noya's shoulder, and Noya slept with his head on the window. Asahi slept sitting perfectly upright with his arms folded, like a monk. Tsukishima had his headphones on and his eyes shut, and Sho couldn't tell if he was asleep. Yamaguchi slept against Sho's arm, which Sho let him do.

Sho watched the window.

The motorway went south through Fukushima and the hills folded away on both sides into paddies and forests and small towns with big shrines. Somewhere around Koriyama he realized he knew this road. Satoshi had done it at 20, on a university team bus going the other way, north, to a tournament in Sendai, the spring before the knee went. He'd sat by the window then, too. He'd watched the same hills go by, and thought about the match, and about his left knee, which had been sore for weeks by then, a little, nothing, nothing he was going to tell anybody about.

He looked at his own knee now, his left one, Sho's, under his track pants. It was fine. It had always been fine.

He took out the notebook and opened it on his knee, and turned to the 2 pages on Nekoma, and then to a blank one, and wrote at the top: *Fukurodani Academy Group. Day 1.* Then he sat with the pen and didn't write anything else, because he didn't know anything else yet. He'd know by tonight.

Past Utsunomiya the land flattened out. The buildings came closer together and got taller and grayer. Around 10 the bus woke up, all at once, the way buses did. Tanaka sat up and wiped his mouth and looked out of the window and said, "Is that Tokyo?"

"That's Saitama," said Ennoshita.

"It looks like Tokyo."

"It's Saitama."

They crossed a river that was wider than any river in Miyagi, brown and slow, with baseball pitches along its banks. Fukurodani Academy had a gymnasium bigger than Karasuno's First and Second Gymnasiums put together.

It was new, or newer, with a curved roof of white steel and glass and high windows all along 1 side, so that the light came down in bright bars across 2 full courts side by side. On 1 court a team in white and black was warming up. On the other, a team in red that Sho knew from May. The noise came off both courts at once and went up into the roof and came back down doubled: shoes squealing, balls slapping, someone laughing, a whistle, a voice shouting "HEY HEY HEY!" at a volume that made 2 of the Karasuno 1st years stop in the doorway.

Sho stopped in the doorway too.

He stood there with his bag on his shoulder and let it all come at him. Ears 1st, the way he'd been training. The pass on the near court, a solid pass off both arms. A set, then a spike, the crack of it doubled by the roof. Somebody's shoes. A pass on the far court, and another, and the roof took the sound of them and threw it back and mixed them together, near court and far court, the echo of 1 pass landing on top of the next one, until he couldn't tell which was which. He shut his eyes, the way he did on the step, and listened.

Everything. All at once. 2 courts and a roof full of echo, and no way to pick 1 pass out of it.

He opened his eyes. Behind him Ennoshita said, "You're in the door," and he moved out of it.

Sho knew the silence. It came the way it always came, without asking: the noise of both courts and the roof and the benches went far away all at once, as if someone had shut a door, and Bokuto became points of light hanging in the air above the net, shoulder and elbow and wrist and hips. The brightest was his right hand, burning white. A ghost peeled off it 1 blink early. Its wrist turned out toward the sideline, and the arm came through long.

Line. And from that height, with that much arm, deep. The back right corner, where Asahi had said he wouldn't hit when he was happy, and where Sho was standing.

He was already moving, 2 steps back and to the right, and down. He put his arms together where the ghost had pointed, and kept them there, and the sound came back all at once, the crack of Bokuto's hand on the ball and the roar off the Fukurodani bench, and the ball was already on him.

It was the hardest ball he'd ever had come at him. Harder than Iwaizumi. It hit his forearms like a kicked door and went straight up, high and wild, toward the side of the court, and Noya ran it down and scooped it over the net with 1 arm.

"NICE, KIYOSE!" Noya yelled, from the floor. "LEAVE THE REST TO ME!"

Fukurodani put it away on the next touch anyway. 1-0 to them.

On the far side of the net Bokuto had landed and was standing quite still, pointing at Sho.

"HEY!" said Bokuto. "13! You got it! You GOT it! That was my best one! Akaashi, did you see? He got my best one!"

Sho went back to his spot. His eyes stung a little, the way your eyes sting after you've stared at something bright, a dull warmth at the back of them. He blinked twice and looked across the net while the Fukurodani server bounced the ball.

After the freeze. 13 had sat on the bench with his hands flat on his knees and looked at nothing, and Kenma had decided then that whatever 13 did came with a cost. A skill with a cooldown. You use it, it goes gray for a while, and you're ordinary until it comes back. Every game had skills like that. You learned how long the cooldown was, and then you hit the player inside it.

So when the next ball came to him from Yaku, Kenma set Tora again, and Tora hit it into the middle of the back row, straight at 13, inside his cooldown.

13 dug it.

He was ordinary about it. No stop. No early. He just moved like a good receiver, saw Tora's shoulder and went where it pointed, and the ball came up to Kageyama clean, and Karasuno scored off it. 14-10.

13 had his hands on his knees, getting his breath back. His eyes were fine. Clear, and calm, and a little interested in the scoreboard.

No cooldown, then. Or one so short Kenma couldn't find it. In May it had taken him the rest of a set to come back.

Kenma found he didn't mind that as much as he'd expected to.

---

Nekoma took the next rally, and Karasuno's coach called a timeout at 15-10.

Nekoma went to their bench. Kenma sat down on the end of it with a towel and drank from his bottle and kept his eyes on the floor, which was how he listened best.

"Their 13," said Nekomata, to nobody in particular, though he was standing in front of Kenma when he said it. "Does he look tired to you?"

Kenma shook his head.

"Hm." Nekomata smiled at the floor. "Lookers get tired. Watching takes it out of you, and that one's watching very hard." He shrugged. "He'll get tired. Later. Not today, maybe."

Kenma drank. He thought Nekomata was a bit wrong this time, and he didn't want to say so, because saying so would mean explaining, and explaining 13 meant explaining something he'd only just started to understand.

He'd seen it twice now. The stop. It had happened yesterday too: he could see it now that he knew what it looked like, 13 in the back row against Fukurodani, Bokuto's arm going back, 13 going still for that little piece of time before he moved for the line. Akaashi must have seen it then. Akaashi saw everything in setters' time. And in May, in the handshake line, Kenma had looked at 13's feet without knowing why. He knew why now. The feet were where it started.

13 went still when he knew where the ball was going.

Guessers kept moving. Guessers shifted and hopped and leaned both ways, because they were waiting to find out. 13 stopped because he'd already found out. The stillness was the moment he decided. After it, he went where he'd decided, and he stayed decided.

Hiding where the ball was going was hard work, and 13 was good at seeing through it. Letting him decide was easy. Then you made him decide wrong.

Kenma lowered the bottle.

"Tora," he said.

Yamamoto turned round so fast he nearly fell over the bench. Kenma didn't talk in timeouts much. When he did, people turned round.

"Next time you're on the left," said Kenma, to the towel, "run in like you're getting it. Louder. Swing all the way. Like it's yours."

Tora looked at him. Then he grinned, a big wicked grin. "So it's not mine."

"Maybe not."

"Got it."

"Kuro. Kai." Kenma still didn't look up. "Run everything. Every time. Even when I'm looking at Tora."

Kuroo's eyebrows went up over the top of his bottle. Kai, who never got excited about anything, nodded once.

The whistle went.

---

Nekoma had rotated on the point before the timeout, and now Kenma was in the front row, on the right, with Tora on the left and Kuroo in the middle. Kai and Fukunaga were in the back with Yaku. Nekoma served.

The right ball came on the 1st rally. Yaku dug a hard one from the shaved one, high and slow, plenty of time, and it came down toward Kenma, and Tora came in from the left.

Tora was always loud. This time he was a stampede, every step slapping the floor, his arms swinging all the way back, his face lit up, his whole body yelling *mine, mine, MINE.* Kai came in from the back row behind him. Kuroo came in up the middle, fast and low, like a man who'd just remembered something. Kenma turned his shoulders to Tora, and looked at Tora, and let the ball come down into his hands at the angle you'd use for a big high set to the left. And he watched 13.

13 was watching his hands. Then, at the edge of it, 13's eyes went to Tora's run, the big loud arm, the shoulder coming round, and his feet went flat and quiet on the floor, and he stopped.

Kenma set the middle.

He pushed it short and quick to Kuroo, who was already in the air because Kenma had told him to be, and Kuroo met it at the top and hit it sharp into the middle of the back row, into the space 13 had just been standing in. 13 was already 2 steps toward the corner where Tora's cross would have gone, if there'd been a cross. Tora had swung his whole arm at nothing, all the way through, and landed laughing.

16-10.

13 stood in the back corner and looked at the place where the ball had landed.

Kenma had been ready for angry. 13 looked at the spot on the floor, and then up at Tora, still grinning, and then across the net at Kenma, straight at him, the way he'd looked at Akaashi's hands in warm-up, with his head tilted a little, as if somewhere in Kenma there was a wire making a noise.

Kenma turned back to the net.

It worked again on the next serve. Tora loud, 13 still, and this time the set went back to Kai, a pipe from the back row up the middle, and 13 was 2 steps the wrong way again, reaching for it with 1 hand, too late. 17-10.

It worked a 3rd time on the serve after that, and this time Kenma didn't set it at all. He let 13 go still on Tora, and then he jumped and pushed the ball over the net with 2 fingers into the front of Karasuno's court, the dump, the oldest trick he had. It dropped in front of Kageyama and Shoyo while both of them were turning to block Tora. Yesterday, against Fukurodani, 13 had called the short ones from the back row; Kenma had heard him across the gym. This one he didn't call. He was 2 steps deep, in Tora's corner, by then.

18-10.

Across the net 13 stood with his hands on his hips, breathing, looking at the floor where the dump had gone. Then he did something Kenma hadn't expected.

He started moving.

On the next rally 13 never stopped. He kept his feet going the whole time, little shuffles, a rock from heel to toe and back, his knees always bending, like a guesser. Like somebody trying very hard to be a guesser.

He'd felt it, then. He'd felt that he was being read through something, and he'd guessed what, or nearly. Kenma was a bit impressed by that.

But you couldn't see what 13 saw and move like a guesser at the same time. When Tora hit a plain cross straight at him, no trick at all, 13 was rocking back on his heels as the ball came, and got to it a beat late, and it went off his arms into the bench. 19-10.

The stillness was where all his early came from. He couldn't throw away 1 without the other.

13 looked down at his own feet.

He stayed on through 1 more of Karasuno's rotations, over to the left side of the back row. He kept rocking. He passed 1 serve well and 1 badly and dug 1 ball from Fukunaga that he'd have been early on an hour ago, and wasn't. Then Karasuno won a rally at 21-12, and the rotation carried his spot to the front, and the big ace came back on for him. 13 walked off, and sat down on the end of Karasuno's bench next to the girl with the camcorder, and put the towel over his knees, and got the pencil out from somewhere, and wrote.

---

Nekoma won 25-17.

Kenma's last set was a plain high ball Tora could hit, and Tora hit it so hard that Karasuno's libero dug it into the wall. Then the whistle went, and everyone shouted, and Lev shouted the loudest about his hand, and Kenma went and sat down on the end of the bench and put the towel over his head.

From under the towel he watched Karasuno line up on the end line for the penalty.

Shoyo went with his face shut tight. He threw himself down the floor as if the floor had done something to him, and got up, and went again, and kept his eyes off Kageyama, who was 2 people behind him doing the same thing with the same face.

13 ran 3 steps and threw himself down. Got up. Down again. On the 3rd one, halfway down the court, he laughed.

It was shorter than yesterday's. It came out of him once, into the floor, and he got up still smiling and went again.

Kenma watched him do it all the way to the far end. He'd lost. He'd been read and made a fool of 3 times in a row by somebody who'd spent the timeout sitting on a bench with a towel. He'd tried something and it had made him worse, and he'd had to come off. And he was lying on the floor laughing.

Kenma didn't have it. He was starting to think he wouldn't get it by watching.

---

The water fountains at Fukurodani were in a corridor outside the gym, 3 of them in a row, low and cold. Kenma liked the end 1. It had the coldest water and nobody used it because it was furthest from the door.

13 was using it.

He was bent over it, cupping water onto the back of his neck. When Kenma came round the corner he straightened up, and wiped his hand on his shorts, and stepped back from the fountain to let him have it, and stayed.

Kenma drank. It took as long as he could make it take.

"When did you see it?" said 13.

His voice was polite. A little amused, the way it had been in May when he'd said *Mostly*. Up close he was tall, nearly as tall as Kuroo, and he stood very easily, the way Nekomata stood, as if he'd been standing for a long time and had stopped thinking about it.

Kenma straightened up. He thought about not answering. 13 would stand there looking at him until he did.

"Yesterday," said Kenma. "Against Fukurodani."

"Did Akaashi-san see it first?"

"Probably."

13 nodded slowly. "I know I stop," he said. "Somebody on my team told me. I don't know what it looks like from over there."

Kenma looked down the corridor, toward the gym door. The small blond girl was standing in it with her clipboard under her arm and the camcorder held against her chest the way you'd hold a cat that might jump. She'd come out to find 13, Kenma thought, and lost her nerve at the door.

"She's been filming you," said Kenma. "All morning."

13 turned and looked at the girl. The girl jumped, and nearly dropped the camcorder, and hid behind the door.

When 13 turned back, he was looking at Kenma the way Kuroo did when Kenma finally said the thing Kuroo had been waiting all day for him to say. Kenma didn't know what to do with that, coming from somebody who wasn't Kuroo. So he stood there and let it be looked at.

"Thank you," said 13.

"That's so you'll stop looking at me," said Kenma.

"Okay."

Kenma started to go. Then he stopped, because he still didn't have it, and it was going to keep him awake again.

"You laughed," he said. "On the floor. Twice."

13 thought about it. Kenma could see him thinking, actually hunting for the true answer, which most people didn't bother to do.

"It was fun," said 13.

"It was a penalty."

"I know," said 13, and smiled, a small one, mostly with his eyes.

Then Lev's voice came down the corridor from the gym door, saying "KENMA-SAN! Yaku-san says we're on in 5 minutes and you have to come or he'll kick me!" and Kenma went, because it was easier.

Behind him he could feel 13 standing by the end fountain, looking at the girl with the camcorder.

---

They played Karasuno again at 2:10. Nekoma won that one 25-20. Shoyo and Kageyama were still not talking, and Shoyo's eyes were still open, and Lev touched 2 more of his quicks and screamed about both of them. 13 came in at the back for the ace again.

He didn't go still once. He didn't rock either. He stood with his feet quiet, an ordinary receiver's feet, and his eyes on Kenma's hands, and he dug what came straight at him and missed what Fukunaga hit behind him, and he was never early at all.

He was being careful. He was playing like an ordinary good receiver, on purpose, so there'd be nothing on him to read until he'd seen for himself what Kenma had been reading.

It was the most boring 13 had ever been.

---

Nekoma went home on the train at 6. They took up most of a carriage. Lev slept with his head against the window and his mouth open, and Yaku sat next to him looking as if he'd like to kick him and was too tired. Tora was telling Inuoka about the stampede, again.

Kenma sat at the end by the door with his console, playing the small man in the field.

Kuroo sat down next to him at Ochanomizu.

"Notebook," he said.

Kenma kept playing.

"You beat him 3 times in a row," said Kuroo. "With 1 timeout. Tora's never been so happy. What was it?"

"He stops," said Kenma. "Before he's right."

Kuroo turned that over. "And you made him right about the wrong guy."

"Yeah."

"And then?"

Kenma's small man walked into a cave. "Then he stopped stopping, and got worse. Then this afternoon he played like nobody."

"So you broke him."

The small man fought a bat. "His manager filmed the whole day," Kenma said. He didn't add that he'd been the one to tell 13 so. Kuroo would find out on his own; Kuroo always did. "He'll find it on the tape."

Kenma killed the bat. He let go of the controller, and the small man stood in the cave and breathed, shoulders going up and down, waiting.

He'd already thought about what came after the tape, somewhere between 2:10 and Ochanomizu. 13 would watch himself stop. Then he'd fix it, or he wouldn't. If he couldn't, he'd be a good receiver who studied setters, and there were lots of those, and Kenma could beat lots of those.

If he fixed it, though. If he turned up at the camp in Saitama with the stop gone, or doing something with it Kenma hadn't thought of yet.

Kenma would rather he fixed it. That was more interesting.

He pressed the button, and the small man went on into the dark.

## Chapter 34: Tape

The bus left Fukurodani at 6:05 on Sunday evening, and for the 1st hour nobody said anything at all.

Most of them would sleep later. For now it was the quiet of a busload of people who'd lost 8 games in 2 days and done 8 lengths of a gym floor on their chests, and who had nothing to say about it that wouldn't make it worse. Tokyo went by the windows in orange lights and train bridges. Tanaka sat with his arms folded and his jaw set, looking at the seat in front of him. Noya had his knees up and his chin on them. Daichi and Suga, in the front row, were talking with their heads close together, the way they did when they were deciding something for everybody.

Hinata was in the 3rd row on the right, by the window, with his forehead against the glass.

Kageyama was in the back row on the left, as far from the 3rd row on the right as the bus allowed.

Sho had the window seat halfway down. Yamaguchi had fallen asleep against his arm at the 1st set of traffic lights, the only one on the bus who could. Sho let him be.

He had his notebook on his knee, open to the page with *Fukurodani Academy Group. Day 1.* at the top, the lines about Akaashi under it, and under those, from this morning, in the pencil he'd carried behind his ear onto the Nekoma bench, 3 more:

*Kenma saw it. Yesterday. Akaashi probably 1st.*

*3 in a row off the same run. I went still on Yamamoto every time. Kuroo, Kai, then the dump.*

*Tried to keep moving. Worse.*

He'd read the 3 lines maybe 20 times since 2:10. He knew what they said. He hadn't seen it.

He closed the notebook and looked out of the window.

---

The bus pulled into Nasukogen service area at 9:20, for the toilets and the vending machines. Ukai went straight to the smoking corner. Takeda went to buy everybody cold barley tea with his own money, which Daichi tried to stop and couldn't.

Sho walked to the end of the car park, where the light from the shop didn't reach and the trucks were parked in a long sleeping row with their engines ticking, and called home.

"You're late."

"We're on the bus. It's 9:20."

"Yesterday was 9:30. Today's earlier. You're late for today." A rustle. The flyer, unfolding on her knees, cheap paper gone soft along its folds. "Okay. Go."

He told her. Nekoma, 17 to 25, in the morning. Ubugawa, 22 to 25. Nekoma again after lunch, 20 to 25. Fukurodani at the end, when everybody was tired, 16 to 25.

"You lost all of them again."

"All 4."

"That's 8." Her pencil scratched. "8 times on the floor."

"8."

"Did you laugh?"

He thought about it. "Once. In the morning."

"Only once?"

"I was busy in the afternoon."

"Busy with what?"

He leaned against a lamp post that had no bulb in it and looked at the trucks.

"Same place. Behind them."

"Less than yesterday."

"Less than yesterday."

Scratch. "Okay. Busy with what?"

The 1st thing on the tape was Fukurodani's ceiling, very bright, for 4 seconds, while Yachi worked out which way up to hold it. Then the floor. Then a corner of Karasuno's bench, and Takeda's knee, and a water bottle. Then the court.

Then him.

He was on the bench, at the end, with the towel on his knees and the pencil behind his ear. The picture rocked a little with Yachi's breathing. He watched himself watch the game: a tall boy sitting very straight, younger than he felt, with Satoshi's old habit of keeping a pencil behind the ear.

The last tape of himself he'd ever watched was a university semifinal, the spring he was 20, and he'd turned it off halfway through. He'd watched other people every day after that, for 9 years. Something over 10,000 hours of them. He'd known what Kenma did with his hands at the top of a set before he'd ever met Kenma, because he'd known what 50 setters like Kenma did with theirs.

He pressed fast-forward. 12-8. The paddle. The ace walking off. Him walking on.

At 13-9 Yamamoto went up on the left, and on the screen, in the middle of the back row, the figure in black with *13* on its back stopped.

Sho paused it, and went forward 1 picture at a time.

There. Yamamoto's arm going back. The figure that was him had been doing what receivers do, little shifts, knees, weight to 1 foot and back. Then, between 1 picture and the next, his heels came down. Both of them, flat, together, as if somebody had cut the strings. Then his knees stopped, then his hands. His head had been still the whole time. His head was always still. 3 pictures of nothing at all, and then he went.

3 frames. About a 10th of a second.

He went back and watched it again, and again. Heels, knees, hands, 3 frames, go. From where Yachi had been sitting it looked like a dog that has heard its name.

He let the tape run and found the next one, and the next, and counted them in the margin, a line for each, the way he used to count a setter's choices for a coach who wanted it on 1 page by 8 a.m.

7 stops while he was on court in the morning, before he'd started trying to hide them.

He went back through the 7 and marked where the ball had gone. 4 times he'd gone where it went. 1 was the dig on Yamamoto's cross, the one with the warm sting afterward. The other 3 he hadn't even noticed at the time; they'd just been him, playing, a step early.

Waiting for Sho's feet.

*Heels down, both at once. Then knees, then hands. Head never moves. 3 frames, then I go.*

*Kenma waits at the top for my heels.*

Then he found the rocking. On the tiny screen the figure with 13 on its back bounced heel to toe, heel to toe, knees going, hands going, all the way through the rally, like a boy outside the headmaster's office who needed the toilet. When Yamamoto's plain cross came, the figure was going back onto its heels at the wrong moment, and reached, and missed.

The 3rd time through he had to put his hand over his mouth.

Then he skipped to the afternoon, the careful game. There was nothing to circle in it. The figure stood with quiet feet and watched the setter and dug what came at it and missed what didn't, and was never early, and never wrong, and never anything.

He knew that figure. He'd fast-forwarded past it 10,000 times. A tidy receiver on a team that lost, whom no coach would ever ask about on a Tuesday morning.

He paused it and found he was leaning closer.

The bus hummed. Outside, the fields had gone completely dark, with a town far off now and then in orange. Most of the bus was asleep at last. Somebody 2 rows back was breathing slow and heavy, Asahi probably. At the front, the only light was the small green clock above the driver's head.

11:04.

Sho looked at his own hands. The camcorder was held up close to his face. His thumb was on the rewind button. His shoulders were up round his ears, his back had come away from the seat, his neck was bent to the little screen, and his eyes were dry from not blinking.

He turned his phone over on his knee. The alarm had gone at 11:00. It said so on the screen: *Alarm dismissed.* He'd turned it off himself, with his thumb, and gone straight back to the tape, and he couldn't remember doing it.

A desk. A dark room. A thumb on a button and a clock in the corner of the monitor saying 3 o'clock, and then saying 3:14.

He took his thumb off the button.

He closed the screen and put the camcorder down on his knee and sat back until he could feel the seat along his whole back. He looked out of the window at the far orange of a town and then at the glass in front of his nose, and then at the town again. Near, far. Near, far. The way Dad held the string at the other end.

Then he opened the notebook and wrote, slowly, sitting back:

*7 stops. 4 right. 3 wrong. The 3 wrong were all Kenma, all after he'd seen it.*

*The stop is where the early comes from. Rocking kills it. Being careful kills it too.*

He looked at that until the letters stopped meaning anything. Then he wrote the line he'd been going round and round since the fountain:

*So keep it. Then what?*

Under it, smaller:

*11:04. Mio said 11.*

He closed the notebook on the pencil and put the camcorder in its case and didn't open either of them again. Across the aisle and down the bus, Yachi's head had fallen sideways onto Kiyoko's shoulder.

## Chapter 35: Ikkei

Hinata came back on Wednesday with a word.

Nobody knew where he'd been on Tuesday evening. He'd left practice early with Coach, the 2 of them getting into the shop's white van in the car park without explaining anything, and on Wednesday morning he came up the hill to the gym at 6:50 saying the word under his breath, over and over, like somebody trying to keep a phone number in his head.

"What's tempo?" said Noya.

"I don't know yet," said Hinata, and went in.

He didn't tell anybody else. He said the word all through Wednesday, to the ball, to the wall, to his own hands in the receive line. He jumped differently, too, in the spike drills, a little later than before, as if he was listening for something before he went. Sometimes it worked and mostly it didn't, and every time it didn't he went straight back to the end of the line without a word to anybody.

He and Kageyama still weren't speaking. They were at practice, both of them, every morning and every evening, just as Sho had told Yachi they'd be, except on Tuesday evening, when Kageyama had left early too, separately, 10 minutes after the van. They worked hard at not speaking. If Kageyama was setting at the near court, Hinata hit at the far one. If they passed each other at the water bottles, they both looked at the bottles.

Sho watched it the way you'd watch weather, and kept out of it.

On Thursday, at the evening water break, Kageyama came and stood next to him.

Sho was sitting against the wall under the window with his bottle. Kageyama stayed standing, with his own bottle in his hand, not drinking from it, looking out at the court where Tanaka and Noya were arguing about a line call.

Sho waited. Kageyama was 1 of those people you had to wait for.

"I saw Oikawa-san," said Kageyama at last.

"Oh?"

"On Tuesday. At the old Coach Ukai's class. The kids' one." Kageyama frowned at the court. "His nephew goes there. He was picking him up."

Tuesday. Sho kept his face where it was. He could see the shape of a story in how stiffly Kageyama was holding the bottle.

"He asked about you," said Kageyama.

"About me?"

"He asked if you still squint." Kageyama finally drank, a short angry swallow, as if the question had been his own fault. "I said I'd never noticed. He said, 'Typical.'"

Sho laughed before he could help it. Kageyama's ears went red.

"Do you?" said Kageyama.

"Squint?"

"Still."

Sho checked, the way he'd check a pass. In June, after Seijoh, he'd squinted at everything till the Wednesday clinic: menus, the board, Mio's handwriting. Now the board at the front of 1-5 was sharp from the back row. Akaashi's hands had been sharp across 2 courts.

"No," he said. "Not anymore."

He was thin, much thinner than the man in the old team photo in Karasuno's club room, the one everybody looked at and nobody talked about. He had a blanket over his knees, in July, and a towel round his neck, and a face that looked as if it had been carved out of something hard and then left out in the weather for 70 years. He was watching the children chase the ball with an expression of total disgust.

"5!" he shouted, in a voice much bigger than his body. "5 of you after 1 ball! Is it a hamster? Is it going to escape? Kota! You're a receiver! Receivers stay home! STAY HOME!"

A small boy in a T-shirt 3 sizes too big ran back to his spot, grinning.

"Gramps," said Ukai, from the door.

The old man looked round. His eyes went to Keishin, and then past Keishin to Sho, and stayed there.

They were sharp eyes, set deep. They went over Sho once, quickly, head to feet: his height, his shoulders, his hands, his knees. Then they came back up and rested on his face. Sho had been looked at like that before, a few times, in the other life, by the 2 or 3 oldest coaches in the V.League, the ones who'd been scouting players since before Satoshi was born. It was the look of somebody pricing you.

"That's him?" said Ukai Ikkei.

"That's him."

"Hm." The old man turned back to the children. "Sit down. Both of you. Class finishes at 7. I'm not stopping for you."

They sat on the low bench by the wall, Sho with his knees almost up to his chin. He watched the old man coach.

He'd watched a hundred coaches on tape in the other life. He'd never watched one teach 8-year-olds. The old man was good. Under the shouting he was the real thing. He saw everything those children did. He saw the girl in the yellow shirt who flinched every time the ball came near her face, and put her at the back, in the corner, where it would only come at her arms. He saw the boy called Kota, who was fast and wild and wanted every ball, and gave him every ball, until Kota's arms were red and he was laughing and didn't care. He coughed, sometimes, a dry cough into the towel round his neck, and kept going.

Sho had been 20, with a ruined knee and everything else still working, and he'd gone and found a room with no windows and stayed in it for 9 years.

He looked down at his own hands, folded on the notebook. Then up at the old man, shouting at a 7-year-old about her feet.

At 7, the parents came. The children ran off to them shrieking about who'd won, and nobody had won, because it hadn't been a game. A mother bowed to the old man and he waved her off like a fly. The girl in the yellow shirt came back from the door to give him a sweet from her pocket, and he took it and told her to go home, and put it in his own pocket, carefully.

Then it was quiet. Just the 3 of them, and the low net, and the lights humming, and the sound of cicadas through the open doors.

Ikkei turned his chair round to face them.

"Keishin," he said. "Get a ball. Go over there. Hit at him."

Ukai looked at him. "I thought you were going to talk to him."

"I'm going to look at him. Then I'll know if he's worth talking to." Ikkei pointed at the court with his chin. "You. Boy. Go and stand there. Middle of the back. Like a receiver. Do what you do."

Sho went and stood there. The net came barely over his head; he could have blocked over it flat-footed. On the other side Ukai picked up a children's ball, soft and light, and stood up on a folding chair so he'd be hitting down from roughly a spiker's height, and looked at his grandfather as if to say *happy now?*

"Hit," said Ikkei.

Ukai threw it up and hit it.

It was an ordinary hit, a coach's hit, cross, at Sho's left. Sho watched Ukai's shoulder and hips and the way his arm came round, and his heels came down, and he went, and the ball came to his arms, and he passed it high back into the middle.

"Again," said Ikkei.

Line. Sho's heels came down; he went; he got it.

"Again."

Short, a tip, off the fingers. Heels; go; he got there with a step to spare.

"Again. Again." Sho, glancing at him between hits, saw where the old man's eyes were: low, on Sho's feet, the whole time.

After 10, Ikkei held up his hand.

"Stop."

Ukai got down off the chair.

Ikkei folded his hands on the blanket. Then he said, to Sho, "What are you looking at?"

"His shoulder, sir. And his hips. Where his arm comes round."

"No. Before that."

"You moved before he hit it," said Ikkei. "3 times out of 10, you were going before his hand touched the ball. Keishin's a terrible hitter, and you were still early on him." He coughed into the towel. "So what were you looking at?"

Sho stood in the middle of the children's court with his hands at his sides, and felt his mouth begin to do the thing that Mio had written down as number 1.

He stopped it.

"I don't know how to say it," he said. "It's true. I don't know."

Ikkei looked at him with those deep sharp eyes, and Sho let himself be looked at.

"Hm," said Ikkei. Then he put his hand out, palm up, toward the notebook on the bench.

"Keishin says you wrote something. Show me."

---

Ikkei read the whole notebook.

He started at the 1st page and turned them over 1 by 1 with a dry thumb, the April pages and the May pages, Nekoma in May with the box drawn round *Kenma*, the pages from Seijoh in June in 2 different handwritings, Suga's round and careful where Sho had been holding a cold pack on his face. He didn't comment. Sometimes he grunted. Once he went back a page to check something. Ukai sat on the bench with his arms folded and the cigarette back behind his ear and looked at the ceiling.

It took a long time. The cicadas went on outside. Sho sat on the bench and kept still.

In the middle of June, Ikkei stopped.

It was the page Sho had written on the train home from the clinic, the 1st page of the new part, with the date at the top and 5 lines under it in his own careful hand:

*Hand up at the burning.*

*Tell Coach 1st.*

*Build the map so I don't need the eye.*

*Listen.*

*Play all 3 sets.*

Ikkei read it, and stayed on it. He didn't turn the page.

Sho found that his left hand had gone to his left knee. It was resting there, on the kneecap, the way Mio had drawn it in her notebook with *LEFT* in capitals. He felt it there and left it, because taking it away now would be worse.

Ikkei's eyes went once from the page to Sho's hand on his knee, and back to the page. Then he turned it over and went on.

At last he got to Sunday's page, and the bottom of it: *So keep it. Then what?*, and under that, smaller, *11:04. Mio said 11.*

He read it twice. Then he laughed.

It was a short, rough laugh, and it turned into a cough halfway through, and he coughed into the towel, and Ukai half stood up, and Ikkei waved him back down.

"Who's Mio?" said Ikkei.

"My sister, sir. She's 10."

"She said 11?"

"She said stop at 11."

"Did you?"

"No," said Sho. "4 minutes late."

Ikkei grunted, and looked at him over the top of the notebook for a beat longer than he'd looked at anything else in it. Then he tapped *Then what?* with 1 finger. "Who read you? Nekomata's setter? The pudding-head Keishin told me about?"

"Kozume-san. Yes, sir."

"Nekomata always did love a boy who watches." Ikkei closed the notebook on his finger, keeping the page. "When I was coaching against him, I scratched my ear before I called a timeout. Here." He touched his right ear. "Every time. Nekomata knew it by our 2nd match. He called his own timeout 1 second before mine, every time, for 40 years, and grinned at me across the gym."

He held up the closed notebook. "So. You stop when you know where the ball's going. You found that out. Good. And you tried hiding it, and it got worse. And you tried being careful, and you got boring." He looked at the page through the closed cover, as if he could still read it. "And you think the problem's the stopping."

Sho waited.

"The stopping's fine," said Ikkei. "Everybody good stops. You can't move right from a wobble. The problem is what you've decided when you stop."

"Where the ball's going."

"Where the ball's going." Ikkei nodded. "1 place. You pick 1 place and you go there, and you're right most of the time, because you're a clever boy who looks hard, and when you're wrong, you're 2 steps the wrong way with your arms out like a scarecrow, and Nekomata's setter has his ball in the floor." Ikkei put the notebook down on his blanket. "Listen. You're looking at where the ball's going to go. Stop that. Look at what can't happen."

Sho looked at him.

"Keishin. Up on the chair." Ikkei didn't look round. "Same as before. Don't hit it. Just get ready to."

Ukai sighed, and climbed back up on the folding chair, and held the children's ball up in his left hand at the top, where a toss would be, and drew his right arm back, and held that too, looking extremely tired of his grandfather.

"Look at him," said Ikkei to Sho. "Tell me where he can't hit it."

Sho looked.

It was a strange thing to be asked. He'd crossed things out for 9 years, on paper: *never sets the pipe from rotation 4*, *never serves short to the libero*. He'd never once crossed anything out with his feet, standing on a court, with a ball about to come. He looked at Ukai's shoulder, turned toward the left; at his hips, square to the net; at his elbow, low; at the ball, a little behind his head.

"He can't hit line," said Sho slowly. "Not hard. His shoulder's turned too far. And he can't hit deep cross, he's too close under it, it'd go out."

"What else."

"He can't tip. His arm's all the way back and his hand's open. He's going to swing."

"So?"

Sho looked at the court. The children's court, small and low, with the line taped on the floor. In his head, without his choosing it, bits of it went dark. The line went dark. The deep cross went dark. The strip just over the net went dark. What was left was a shape, a lopsided strip across the middle of the court, short cross to the middle of the back.

"It's somewhere in there," said Sho.

"So stand in there," said Ikkei. "Keishin. Hit it."

Ukai hit it, short cross. Sho was already in the strip, and he took a half step, and the ball came to him, and he passed it, and his heels had come down, he felt them, but they'd come down somewhere that was right for 3 different balls instead of 1.

"Again," said Ikkei. "And this time do it in your head before he's at the top."

They did it 10 more times. It was slow. It was terribly slow. Sho could feel his head working like an old computer, crossing out 1 thing and then another while the ball was already on its way, and twice he hadn't finished crossing out before Ukai hit it, and he went for a ball he'd already ruled out and missed it. Once he crossed out the wrong thing entirely, and the ball went straight into the place he'd decided it couldn't, and he stood there looking at it.

"Ha," said Ikkei, and clapped his hands once.

"He couldn't hit that from there," said Sho.

"He's a terrible hitter. Terrible hitters do things that can't happen. So do good ones, sometimes." Ikkei pulled the blanket up on his knees. "It'll be slow for a month. Maybe 2. Then it won't be. Then you'll do it without words, the way you do the other thing, whatever that is."

"And stop looking at everything like you're counting change in a shop," said Ikkei. "I watched you watch my class. Your eyes went everywhere. Flick, flick, flick. Look at the middle. The setter, the middle of the net, something. Let the edges come to you. They'll come. You don't have to go and fetch them."

Sho stood in the middle of the children's court, and tried it, there and then: his eyes on the middle of the low net, on the tape, and the edges of the gym lying round it, Ukai on his chair, the bench, the open door, all there at once without his going to look at any of them. It made him dizzy. It made the edges blurry and wide.

"Slow," said Ikkei. "Also for a month. Go home. You're tiring me out."

---

They were at the door, Ukai jingling the van keys, when Ikkei spoke again behind them.

"Keishin."

Ukai turned round.

"That one sees the hit before the hitter does." Ikkei was sitting in his folding chair with the blanket over his knees, unwrapping the sweet the little girl had given him, with his eyes on the wrapper. "Bring him back after the camp. I want to see if he's done it."

Ukai looked at his grandfather. Then at Sho.

"Yeah," he said. "Okay."

Sho bowed from the door, low, the full bow he used for Mom's parents at New Year.

"Thank you very much, sir."

Ikkei put the sweet in his mouth and flapped a hand at him.

---

It was dark by the time the van got back onto the main road. The rice had gone black on both sides, and the headlights of the cars coming the other way came and went across the windscreen.

Ukai drove back with both hands on the wheel, which he never did, and the cigarette stayed behind his ear.

"*Look at what can't happen,*" he said, at the next junction. "He said that to me when I was 12. About blocking. I've been working out what it means for 14 years."

"Do you know now?"

"Some of it." Ukai turned off toward Karasuno. He glanced at Sho in the dark, a short sideways look, and back at the road. "*Sees the hit before the hitter.* What's that about?"

Sho looked out of the windscreen.

"I see things early," he said. "It's IQ."

Ukai's head turned. "You heard that?"

"You said it to Takeda-sensei. In the gym. Before exams."

"Hm." A car passed them, and then another. "Annoying old man," said Ukai, to the road.

The car in front of them was a small blue one, in the left lane, with its indicator off.

Sho looked at it. Before he'd decided to, his head did the new thing, slowly. It can't turn right: wrong lane, no signal. It can't stop: there's nobody behind it, the light's green. It's going straight, or left into the petrol station.

It went left, into the petrol station.

He tried it on the next car. A white van, a delivery van like Ukai's. It can't turn, it's too big for that road. It can't speed up, there's a bend. It went straight, slowly.

He did it all the way back to Karasuno, car after car, at every junction, while Ukai drove with both hands and the radio off. He got most of them. It was slow, and his head ached a little behind the forehead the way it did after Classical homework, and it was the most fun he'd had all week.

At the last set of lights before the school a taxi in front of them did a complete U-turn across 2 lanes, without signaling, in a place where no car could possibly turn.

Sho laughed out loud.

"What?" said Ukai.

"Taxi," said Sho.

Ukai looked at the taxi, and at Sho, and shook his head, and the light went green.

He pulled up at the school gate at 8:20, by the dead lamp post, and Sho turned on the little light in the van's roof and opened the notebook on his knee to Sunday's page. Under *So keep it. Then what?* he wrote:

*Look at what can't happen. Stand in what's left.*

*Slow for a month. (He said maybe 2.)*

Then, under that, because Takeda had pinned the camp letter to the club room door that morning and he'd counted the days on the way out:

*Saitama: 16 days.*

Ukai leaned over and read it upside down, and grunted, and turned the roof light off.
