## Part A. What the research actually says

### A1. Experts read earlier, and from the body, not the ball
- **Expert vs intermediate gap is biggest just BEFORE contact.** In UBC occlusion work (Hodges lab, Dhaliwal MSc), clips of a setter were cut at points around ball contact. At 60 ms before the setter's contact, expert setters and blockers were about 65-70% accurate at predicting the set; intermediates about 45-50%. At contact the gap shrank (everyone can see the ball leave by then). Viewpoint (setter's view vs blocker's view) mattered less than predicted. Outside hitters were worse at reading sets than setters and blockers: reading the setter is a learned, role-specific skill.
- **Wright et al.:** the last ~167 ms before the setter's contact, up to and including contact, gave experienced players usable information that novices ignored.
- **Bordini et al. 2015 (spike, temporal occlusion):** clips cut 399, 266, 133 ms before the spike contact, at contact, and 133 ms after. Correct "where is the spike going" calls rose from about 36% at the earliest cut to about 98% at the latest. Experienced adults were better than less experienced groups mainly at the EARLY cuts. (Takeaway: about 0.4 s before contact the attack is close to a coin toss even for good players; around 0.13 s before, experts already know much of it.)
- **Beach volleyball:** players and coaches (who have motor experience of the action) beat expert referees (who only watch) at the latest occlusion point. Doing the skill, not just watching it, builds the read. (Satoshi was a hitter for years: his reads of HITTERS should be stronger than his reads of setters or blockers, which he never played.)
- **Schorer et al. 2015:** foveal and peripheral fields both feed attack prediction, and different skill levels use different sources of information.
- **Starkes & Allard 1983:** volleyball players detect the ball in a flashed game photo faster and more accurately than non-players, but only in real game structure. The classic "pattern recall" finding (Allard, Graham & Paarsalu 1980, basketball): experts recall positions better ONLY when the scene is a structured play. Random scenes, no advantage. A mapped brain is a pattern library, not a camera.

### A2. Where elite eyes go (gaze studies)
- **Experts watch the setter; novices watch the ball.** Vansteenkiste et al. (blocking task, female players) and Zhu et al. 2024 (14 elite, 13 semi-elite, 11 novices, eye tracking): experts focus on setter and spiker; novices chase the ball's whole flight.
- **Fewer, longer fixations.** Piras et al. 2014 and Zhu 2024: experts make fewer fixations of longer duration. Piras: experts look at the start of the pass trajectory, then jump to the setter's HANDS, ignoring the rest of the ball's flight. Non-athletes follow the whole ball before and after the hands.
- **Visual pivots and peripheral vision.** Vansteenkiste 2015 (Biology of Sport): all groups used "visual pivots" at reception and the final pass; accuracy was elite 97.5%, intermediate 91.5%, novice 83.5% (with huge novice variance). Authors suggest experts extract more from parafoveal/peripheral regions: they park the eyes at one anchor point and read the rest of the court around it.
- **Blockers:** gaze on ball plus setter's WRISTS predicted set direction better than looking at the setter's head (head can lie; wrists can't hide as well). Vila Maldonado 2012: "ball-wrist" was the most repeated gaze path; strategies differ for standing vs jump sets. Japanese study (Nara Women's Univ.): while judging the toss course, skilled blockers kept gaze on the setter's FEET or body.
- **Viewpoint matters:** on-court perspective produced more fixations focused on the spiker's detailed movement than a stands/broadcast view (Zhu 2024). A video analyst's broadcast-angle brain must be retrained for the eye-level view. This is a real problem Satoshi would have.
- **Serve reception:** Japanese 2010 study (6 experts, 6 novices, mobile eye trackers): experts look at the SERVER'S SHOULDER AND ARM during the toss, then pick up the ball in flight faster. Another Japanese group: experienced receivers extract lateral direction of the serve BEFORE hand-ball contact. Tennis return: experts fixate the server's upper body and ball longer; novices scatter gaze off the body.
- Caveat: small samples, mostly video lab tasks, results not uniform (one review says expert "fewer fixations" depends on task).

### A3. Quiet eye
- Quiet eye (Vickers) = the final fixation on a key location before a critical movement starts; elite performers hold it longer and steadier. Vickers worked on lengthening passers' quiet eye for serve reception.
- Volleyball quiet-eye training studies (mostly small Iranian samples, beginners/children): QE training beat technique-only training for forearm serve reception learning and serving.
- Under ANXIETY, quiet eye shortens and gaze becomes "stimulus-driven": more, shorter fixations jumping to threatening things (Wilson, Vine & Wood 2009, basketball free throws; penalty kickers stare at the keeper). Elite biathletes who LENGTHENED quiet eye under competition pressure resisted collapse (Vickers & Williams 2007).
- Fiction use: Sho's "settle" ritual before reading a serve (one long fixation on the server's hitting shoulder) is real; panic or the eye firing makes his gaze scatter.

Sources: https://brieflands.com/journals/jmcl/articles/144164 ; https://doaj.org/article/3953f233a1c04fdd94385dfe651999a4 ; https://bigthink.com/neuropsych/quiet-eye-training/ ; https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/9606 ; https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/9546 ; https://medicine.exeter.ac.uk/phss/research/psychology/quieteye/

### A4. Coaching "eye sequences" (how reading is actually taught)
- **Blockers: Ball, Setter, Ball, Hitter (BSBH).** Start on the ball (the pass), switch to the setter as the ball arrives, back to the ball as it leaves to see the set, then to the hitter. Some coaches add a Hitter step first ("find the hitters as the ball crosses the net"). Drills have players SAY the sequence aloud. Hebert (Thinking Volleyball): final read happens between setter release and hitter contact, around 1.4 s or less.
- **Defenders (diggers): a different sequence that includes the block.** Mark Lebedew: Ball, Blockers, Ball, Spiker (single block). The defender reads where his own block is, then takes the "window" the block leaves.
- **Defenders stop at contact.** Be still when the hitter swings; moving early forces a reversal. Take "one last close look at the hitter before contact". Assume a full swing unless the arm lift says tip.
- **Blocker tip:** look right at the setter's hands; jump slightly after the setter's release (read, not guess).
- **Read vs commit block.** Commit = the middle jumps with the quick hitter before the set is seen (no reaction time needed, but beaten by any other set). Read = wait, see the set, then move (late on fast tempo). A 2006 match-analysis of women's play: in over half of attack sequences the middle attacker was NOT actually available for the quick, which is why reading pays off when the pass is bad. Lobietti et al. ran 3D kinematics on both.

### A5. Reading hitters (defense/libero lore)
- Hitters tend to hit where they approach and where their shoulders face; cross-court approach vs line approach narrows options.
- Arm swing: full backswing pulling the hand behind the head = full swing; the hand staying visible in front through the lift = tip or off-speed. High fast arm = power; slow deliberate = cut or roll.
- Watch elbow height, approach speed, shoulder direction. Blockers line up on the HITTING shoulder.
- Diggers square feet and hips to the hitter, drop the inside shoulder, angle the platform to the middle.
- Know each hitter's power shot and tendency AHEAD of time (scouting).

Sources: https://usavolleyball.org/resource/so-you-want-to-be-a-great-defender-learn-to-read-the-hitter/ ; https://usavolleyball.org/resource/five-tips-for-better-backcourt-defense/ ; https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/arm-swing-reading-drill-back-bent-straight/ ; https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/see-the-attacker-drill/ ; https://www.volleyballireland.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/LTPD-Defence-card.pdf ; https://www.swimoutlet.com/guides/defensive-tips-for-beach-volleyball/

### A6. Reading setters (blocker lore)
- Setter well behind the ball tends to set outside (front); neutral/under the ball = middle or back; a dropped setting arm or early left hand = dump threat.
- First clue = setter's position relative to the ball: ball in front of the forehead makes a back set hard; ball overhead or slightly behind forces you to read shoulders and body.
- Front set: feet stop under the ball, body faces target, release in front of the head. Back set: hips push forward, back arches.
- Check shoulders and hips before the setter touches the ball (quick vs high ball).

### A7. Hearing the ball (supports Sho's ch 27-28 "reading passes by sound")
- **Volleyball-specific (Sors, Camponogara et al., Trieste):** volleyball players classify serves as short or long from the IMPACT SOUND better than non-athletes and soccer players; real recorded sound beats spectrum-altered sound; volleyball players use the frequency spectrum (timbre), soccer players mostly loudness. With serves, early auditory cues were MORE salient than matching visual cues for predicting landing zone (amateurs). Participants judged smash speed better from the impact sound than from the visual impact.
- **Tennis (Cañal-Bruland et al. 2018, 2022):** louder contact sound makes players predict a longer, faster ball. But the effect depends on context: replace the racket sound with a sine tone and the location effect vanishes. Grunts also bias predictions.
- **Table tennis:** removing sound impaired perception-action coupling, though sound was not the main cue.
- Fiction use: sound is real but secondary and FOOLABLE (a loud pass is not always a good pass; a crowd or a gym echo distorts it). Sound is also the cheapest channel: it costs no eye movement, so it's the perfect "load-free" input when Sho's eyes are burning.

### A8. Scanning (the "neck check")
- Jordet (football): the 10 s before receiving the ball; players grouped by scans per second. High scanners (0.41-1.8 scans/s) completed 17% more passes and 33% more forward passes in the opposition half than low scanners (0-0.2). Lampard 0.62, Gerrard 0.61. Over 0.5 scans/s is "very high". Season-long follow-up: scanning has a small but positive effect; match demands limit how much even elite players can scan. Headband sensor study: more and wider head turns before receiving = more turning with the ball and more forward passes.
- Volleyball translation [derived]: volleyball has natural dead time before each serve (about 8 s whistle window) and between touches. A Meta Vision "snapshot of all 12" = 2-3 head turns pre-serve; mid-rally scanning is only possible during slow phases (high ball, free ball, out-of-system dig).

### A9. The cost of reading: fatigue, pressure, overload
- **Mental fatigue** (badminton): more fixations, shorter fixations, slower and less accurate decisions. In other words fatigue turns an expert's gaze back into a NOVICE'S gaze. Soccer: 30-min Stroop task lowered passing decision accuracy and slowed responses.
- **Physical exhaustion** (maximal aerobic work): decisions got FASTER but more wrong. (Tired Sho guesses early.)
- **Anxiety:** quiet eye shortens, gaze jumps to threats (A3).
- **Deception:** experts are not immune. Basketball shot fakes fooled experts at late points; best anti-fake strategy was fixating the hips and legs, not the head. Soccer penalties: KICKERS (experts at the action) were MORE often fooled by incongruent actions than goalkeepers and novices: knowing the action deeply makes you trust its tells. Review (2024): deceit (false info) vs disguise (hiding info); little is known about disguise. Fiction gold: Sho, the over-reader, is more baitable than a dumb blocker. Kunde et al. 2011 found head-fake (looking one way, passing another) slows observers even when they know it's coming [memory].

### A10. Training that works (and the catch)
- **Meta-analysis:** video-based temporal occlusion training gives large improvements in anticipation that transfer to lab AND field tests.
- **THE CATCH (volleyball, Spanish youth blockers):** video-only training made players FASTER but not more ACCURATE on court. Only the mixed group (video + on-court practice) improved field success rate. Cricket: occlusion + motor practice beat occlusion alone (earlier pickup of advance info). Tennis: sequential practice helped transfer. Football corners: progressive occlusion training improved pro players' real match performance.
- Schorer et al. ("Hastening the acquisition of perceptual skill in volleyball players"): positive effects, but acquisition is complex.
- This is the single best real-world fact for the arc: **Satoshi's film brain alone makes him quick, not right. Camp volume (live reps against the same teams) is what converts video knowledge into on-court accuracy.**

### A11. Scouting into cues (the analyst's job)
- Teams and setters become patterned: chart set distribution PER ROTATION (rotation wheel with set %), outside hitters' tendencies after they PASSED vs didn't pass, middles' tendencies when starting behind vs in front of the setter, who gets the ball in vs out of system, the go-to hitter "in trouble". Players should build their own shot charts.
- Tools: Hudl attack tendency lines/distribution, setter tendencies by player; Setter Analyzer filters by rotation, reception quality (A/B pass), score range, decoy type. (Data Volley is the pro standard [memory]: every touch coded with skill, type, quality grade, zone, combination code; analysts hand coaches setter distribution by rotation x pass quality and attack direction charts.)
- Elite-level papers: reception quality shapes the attack (2000 Olympics analysis); a Russian Super League study: setter watches the opposing server to guess serve style and target "by the first signs".
- The translation step: a scouting report gives PRIORS ("Rot 1, bad pass: 70% to the 4"). On court, the cue updates the prior ("pass is perfect, setter's under it, middle is in the air: the 70% is now 30%"). Reading = prior x live cue.

### A12. The time budget [derived from typical speeds + general vision science, memory]
- One fixation + saccade costs roughly 250-330 ms; realistic ceiling 3-4 deliberate looks per second.
- High school jump serve (80-100 km/h) over ~15 m: about 0.55-0.7 s. Float (60-75 km/h): about 0.8-1.1 s, and it moves late.
- Spike (high school boys 70-90 km/h; elite women measured ~71 km/h; elite men 100+): attacker to digger 6-9 m = about 0.25-0.45 s. Simple visual reaction ~0.2 s. So a digger CANNOT react to the ball off the hand; he must be in place from cues read before contact.
- High outside set: about 1.2-1.5 s from setter to hitter = 4-5 looks (full BSBH possible).
- Quick (A) set: about 0.3-0.5 s = 1-2 looks. You cannot read setter AND hitter. You commit or you're late. Hinata/Kageyama's freak quick is, scientifically, unreadable: the only answer is a prior (commit), which is what Sho's map would tell a blocker.
- This explains in-world why the Emperor's Eye's "0.2 s ghost" is a cheat: it delivers in one look what experts barely extract in the 60-170 ms before contact.

## Part C. Realistic limits (use these to keep it honest)

1. **Speed wall.** A quick attack gives 1-2 looks. You can't read setter AND hitter. You can only commit by prior. (Hinata's freak quick and the new quick are scientifically unreadable live; the map's job vs them is probability, not sight.)
2. **The ball after contact can't be read better than physics.** A good float moves late and unpredictably. The answer is posture, platform and the server's toss/contact sound, not "seeing" the wobble.
3. **Disguise.** Elite setters take every ball at the same point and give no honest head; their wrists are the last thing left. Oikawa, Akaashi, Kenma, Atsumu. Kenma (who reads readers) is a natural counter.
4. **Expert bias / bait.** Players who know an action deeply are MORE fooled by incongruent actions (soccer kickers). Sho, who trusts tells, is baitable; Kuroo's read block or Akaashi's eyes could farm this.
5. **Reading isn't moving.** Block E, Reception D. Knowing where the ball goes doesn't put his hands there. Reads should often be SPOKEN to teammates (he already called Seijoh's attacks for blockers in ch 22) because they can execute what he can't.
6. **Overload looks like a novice.** Too many targets = many short fixations, slower and wrong (same signature as mental fatigue). The "freeze" vs Nekoma is real.
7. **Fatigue modes.** Mental fatigue: slower, less accurate. Physical exhaustion: FASTER but wrong (early guesses). Anxiety: gaze jumps to the threat (stares at Bokuto, misses the setter dumping).
8. **New team, empty map.** Pattern advantage exists only for structured, familiar patterns. Fukurodani day 1 = Sho reads like a good high-schooler, not an analyst.
9. **Viewpoint change.** His 9 years were broadcast/end-line camera angles. On-court eye level is a different picture; studies show different fixations by viewpoint. Early misreads should come from this.
10. **Video alone makes you fast, not right.** Satoshi's tape habits produce early, confident, sometimes wrong moves until live reps calibrate them.
11. **Sound is context-bound.** A different gym (Shinrinkoen's gym, a packed arena later) changes the echo; loud contact biases toward "long/fast".
12. **Eye cost** [memory]: heavy sustained convergence and tracking strain is real (asthenopia); the doctor's "exhausted convergence" matches. Peripheral/"soft eyes" reading costs less than foveal staring, which gives Sho a real reason to learn to read WITHOUT looking hard.

## Part D. What training builds it (camp-ready drills, all real)

- **Temporal occlusion video:** pause tape at -400/-260/-130 ms, call the set or shot, then check. Satoshi's old job turned into a drill; works with Yachi/Takeda filming on a phone. Must be paired with live reps (A10).
- **Live "call it" drills:** blockers shout "Ball, Setter, Ball, Hitter" (BSBH) or call the set at the setter's release; diggers shout "line/cross/tip" at the hitter's arm lift.
- **"See the attacker" and arm-swing reading drills:** a coach on a box varies tip vs swing; diggers call it before contact.
- **3-ball wash / read-block reps** for middles (Tsukishima's arc lives here; Sho can feed reads, not steal the read block).
- **Neck-check scanning:** count head turns before each serve and each set; target over 0.5 scans/s in dead time.
- **Quiet eye routine:** one long fixation on the server's hitting shoulder from toss to contact; lengthen under pressure.
- **Eyes-shut passing ID:** name passer, arm and quality from sound (already in-story).
- **Self-made shot charts and rotation wheels:** per opponent, per rotation, per pass quality.
- **Stillness at contact:** stop, read, then move.
- **Soft-eye pivot drill:** fix on the setter, call the hitter's approach from peripheral vision.

## Part E. How a learned map would realistically grow over a summer of camp volume

### Volume estimate [derived]
- 1 set ~ 40-50 rallies. A camp day of practice matches ~ 5-8 sets. First Tokyo trip (Golden Week/weekend-scale, ~2-3 days) + main summer camp (~1 week) ~ 50-80 sets total.
- Rallies watched or played: about 2,500-3,500. Each rally has 3-6 readable events (serve, pass, set, attack, dig, cover). Total: roughly 10,000-15,000 read-and-check events, plus penalty dives as a real cost marker.
- A crude setter table needs 6 rotations x 2-3 pass qualities = 12-18 cells; ~20 sets per cell to trust it = 250-350 setter decisions. Playing a team 8-15 sets gives 300-600 of that setter's sets. **By camp's end, Sho can honestly have a usable table for Kenma and Akaashi; only a thin one for Ubugawa and Shinzen; zero for Inarizaki, Shiratorizawa, etc.** That's a realistic map: deep where he has volume, empty where he doesn't.

### Stages (realistic, earned)
1. **Week 0-1 (first camp): Raw film brain on court.** Too many fixations, chases the ball, broadcast-angle habits. Video-trained speed without accuracy: moves early, wrong. Freeze on multi-attack tempo. Learns the "pass start then setter" jump (Piras pattern).
2. **Mid-summer: Chunking.** "Pass quality -> setter options" becomes one unit. Fewer, longer fixations. Starts reading the set's flight for what the hitter CAN'T do (cue 17) instead of guessing what he will do. Sound covers the pass so eyes go to the setter earlier.
3. **Second camp, early: Priors per team.** Rotation wheels in his head (and notebook): "Fukurodani Rot 1 out of system: Bokuto 80%". Calls for blockers become probabilistic, and Tsukishima uses them his own way (protected arc). The map gets beaten by disguise (Akaashi) and by the speed wall (Hinata/Kageyama new quick, Fukurodani tempo).
4. **Second camp, late: Peripheral pivot.** Parks eyes on the setter, sees hitters in the periphery, updates the map every touch, but ONLY in slow phases (high ball, free ball, out-of-system). This is a believable seed of Field map Lv1, with fast tempo still beyond it. Reading costs less because he looks less hard: the in-world reason the eye fires less when the map is good ("Build the map so I don't need the eye").
5. **End of summer: Counter-read.** Others start reading HIM (Kenma, Kuroo, Akaashi notice he goes still and looks at 1 player). Fatigue tells and bait reads start to matter; his weapon now has a known shape, which is the real price of being good at it.

### Mapping research to the 2 powers
- **Meta Vision** = everything in Parts B-E: learned, data-limited, prior x cue, scales with volume, weak to disguise and speed, costs attention and degrades with fatigue like real expert gaze.
- **Emperor's Eye** = a supernatural overclock of the real expert window (60-170 ms before contact, the kinematic chain of hips, shoulder, wrist). Its "brightest point = limb about to act" matches where experts actually look (wrists, hitting shoulder, hips). Its cost (convergence exhaustion, lost depth) is the real eye strain of sustained foveal tracking pushed past limits. The realistic design rule: the eye is strongest exactly where Meta Vision is weakest (quick tempo, disguised setters, new teams), and Meta Vision is strongest where the eye adds nothing (slow phases, familiar opponents, sound).
