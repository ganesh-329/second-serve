# 05: Power system design for Arc 3 (and the road to arcs 4 to 6)

Agent 5 of 10. Scope: exact rules of Emperor's Eye in arc 3, Meta Vision substages, how the 2 interact, named weapons, stat progression, pitfalls and secrecy.

Everything in sections marked **Author mechanics** is for the author and the planning docs only. Sho never learns it in those words, and the prose never explains it. The prose shows it through the body (eyes "parked" or "jumping", the thumbnail crescent, red eyes, the silence) and through 1-line notebook rules.

---

## 0. The 1-paragraph version

Arc 3's power thesis: **the map has to go quiet.** Every cost Sho has paid so far came from the eye firing while his head was full of words (scanning, narrating, holding the notebook map). The eye is cheap when the rest of his vision is calm and expensive when it is busy. So in arc 3 he doesn't train the eye at all (he can't). He trains a wordless map: ears for his own side (the sound reading from ch 27 to 28), a parked "wide gaze" for the far side (Ikkei's lesson), memory for everything else. When that map exists, the eye stops being the engine and becomes "the extra": it fires less often, costs a sting instead of a burn, and he learns the 1 real skill of the arc, **the switch** (drop the map, take the ghost, let it go, rebuild by ear). He does this once, cleanly, in the Fukurodani final, and still loses 25-23. Card: Meta Vision Scanning to **Half map**. The eye stays **Partial awakening** all arc, but late in camp it starts giving "embers" (silence and a lit point with no ghost), the first tremor of the Inarizaki awakening.

## 1. Emperor's Eye in arc 3: exact rules

Established lore lines: it fires "when the play outran his brain... when he'd stopped narrating and just moved" (ch 9); "Don't ask it. Be ready for both." (ch 15); "Don't run both." (ch 14); "The eye shows where. The body has to get there." (ch 6). In ch 22 at 22-21 he "reached for it and there was nothing": even during the fusion he could **hold** a fire, never **start** one.

### 1.2 The core rule: it can't open the door, it can hold it open

- **Sho can never start a fire before Inarizaki.** Asking, staring, wanting: nothing (ch 6, ch 15, ch 22 at 22-21).
- **Once a fire has started, he can hold it** (keep the outline up past the ghost and into the next action). That is what ch 22 was. Holding is the expensive act. The fire itself is cheap or not depending on load (below).
- **The only brake is his eyelids.** If he shuts his eyes or drops them to the floor when the silence starts, the picture doesn't form. Useful on the bench and right after a hand-up. Useless mid-rally.

### 1.3 Triggers (author mechanics): 3 gates, all needed

1. **Speed gate.** The target's action is faster than Sho's model can resolve: quick attacks, jump serves, full-power spikes, a setter's last-instant decision, a dig he can't read normally. Slow, readable play (a free ball, a high outside set to a plain hitter) never fires it.
2. **Lock gate.** His attention is on **1 player** at the instant that player acts. The eye fires on whoever he is locked on, and only them. (This is why Kenma can beat it in arc 3 by choosing who Sho locks on.)
3. **Silence gate.** He has stopped narrating. The fire comes in the half-second when he is committed and out of words: the play has outrun the words. It never comes while he is asking for it or while he is calm and chatting to himself about the rally.

**Modifiers (raise the odds, never guarantee):**
- **Marked players** (see Weapon W2): players he has studied for hours fire more readily and give a cleaner ghost. Arc 1-2 proof: Hinata (watched since April) fired it twice in his 1st game; Oikawa (starred in the notebook, studied all of day 1) fired it on the 1st serve he took.
- **Stakes for Sho personally:** a ball he must play, a call he must make. Bench fires happen (ch 18) but are rarer and cost almost nothing.
- **Overload** (the scan has outrun itself) is a trigger in its own right: ch 14. That's why a narrated map makes the eye fire at the worst moment.

### 1.4 What it shows, exactly

- **Sound:** the gym goes silent (muffled, as if a door shut) for the length of the fire, about 0.3 to 0.5 s of real time.
- **The target:** a skeleton of points of light (head, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles). Everyone else drops to gray. The **brightest point** is the limb about to act (hitting hand for a spiker or server, both hands for a setter, the hands for a blocker).
- **The ghost:** peels off that limb and the body behind it about **0.2 s early** and traces the action: the swing's path and direction, the set's direction, the block's slide. Sho infers the ball's line from the swing (ch 22: "the line it drew pointed at his own right hip").
- **Afterglow:** the outline fades over 1 to 2 s, "like the afterimage of a bright window". **The temptation to hold lives in the afterglow.** This is where every bad decision happens.

**What it never shows (hard limits, useful as counters):**
- **Physics after contact.** A float serve's wobble (the server doesn't know where it will dance, so the body can't show it), net cords, deflections off the block, tooled balls, a ball shanked by Sho's own teammate. The eye reads intention in a body. It can't read a ball.
- **Anyone but the locked target.** While it fires, the rest of the court is gray. Sho is blind to the map for that half-second (the "blind spot" he jokes about later, after Neji's Byakugan).
- **More than 0.2 s.** No growth in arc 3. Ever. (0.5 s is the Inarizaki line.)

**Information value depends on the target's body (new, consistent with ch 18):**
- **Honest bodies give a blank fire.** Aone: ghost and man in the same place. The eye only pays where a body hides or delays its intent (feints, late decisions, disguised setters) or moves faster than reading allows.
- **Raw bodies give a noisy ghost.** A player with no fixed technique (Lev) produces a ghost that wobbles or doubles, because his own body hasn't decided. Comic and useful: the eye is worst on the rawest player at camp.
- **Clear, committed bodies give the cleanest ghost.** Hinata (pure commitment) and Bokuto (huge, decisive swings that change at the last moment) are the eye's best targets at camp.

### 1.5 The cost curve: load times speed

This is the rule that reconciles every fire so far:

**Cost = how fast the target was x how busy Sho's vision was (load), plus anything he holds.**

| Load at the moment of the fire | Slow / honest target | Fast target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Zero** (bench, eyes resting, watching) | Nothing but the thumbnail crescent | **Sting** |
| **Low** (2 checks, feet ready; later the quiet map) | Sting | **Sting** (ch 15 Yamamoto dig) |
| **High** (scanning, narrating, holding the notebook map) | Burning | **Burning on the 1st fire** (ch 5, ch 22) |
| **Any + HOLD** past the afterglow | Pressure after 1 to 3 rallies; depth by about 4 to 6; nosebleed at about 8 (ch 22) | same, faster |

The steps, in Sho's body:

| Step | What he feels | What it does to his play | Recovery (arc 3) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sting** | Dry warmth, like wind in the eyes; 1 or 2 blinks clear it | Nothing | 1 to 2 rallies (30 to 60 s) |
| **Burning** | Hot, dry, "staring into a light"; doesn't clear with blinking | Nothing yet, which is the trap | Off court, eyes shut, cold towel: 10 min at the start of arc 3, about 5 to 6 min by the end (convergence training, 1.7) |
| **Pressure** | A thumb behind the eyes, then a fist, then a nail | Headache; reaction feels a hair late | Rest of the day off; no screens that night; check in the morning |
| **Depth** | Lines double, faces "flat like a photo", short balls misjudged | Misses free balls, mis-times blocks (ch 22 at 21-21) | Overnight to 2 days; doctor visit |
| **Nosebleed** | - | Out of the match | Days; and per ch 28, a **same-day MRI** if it happens "like Sunday" again |

**Combination rules (arc 3):**
- **2 stings inside 3 rallies = 1 burn.** This is Sho's own notebook rule, written after ch 38 (see 1.6), because he catches himself calling a burn a sting.
- A 2nd fire **while still burning** jumps straight to pressure.
- Arc 3 **never** goes past burning on the page. Pressure appears once at most, as the thing he stops before. Depth and nosebleed are the arc 2 memory and the arc 5 threat (Kamomedai).

### 1.6 The hand-up rule, made exact

What's in force: right hand flat at shoulder height the second the burning starts; Ukai takes him off; no questions in front of anyone, "especially if we're winning". Tell Coach 1st. Tell Mom the same day. Tell Mio after, all of it.

Rigorous consequences the chapters should use:

1. **Volleyball's substitution rule makes the hand cost a set.** A substitute can enter once per set and can only be replaced by the starter he replaced [memory, FIVB rule 15.6; the bible already cites the FIVB rules PDF for the libero rule]. Sho almost always enters as a sub for Asahi or Tanaka. **So when the hand goes up, he's out for the rest of that set.** Camp practice matches may run loose subs; recommend Ukai keeps official rules for Sho on purpose: "In January it costs the set. So it costs the set now." (Say it in Ukai's plain words, once.)
2. **The return test (new, Ukai's, no explanation given):** after the set break or 1 full rotation, Ukai holds up a finger at arm's length and moves it: "Touch." Sho touches it 3 times; then reads the far scoreboard flip. Pass both and the burning's gone: he can go back in the next set. Fail and he's done for the day. (Finger touching is a plain depth check; it gives a repeatable visual beat with no lecture.)
3. **2 hands in 1 day = done for the day.** Ukai's rule, set in ch 38.
4. **Stings are not a hand.** But they are reported: at the next set break, 1 line to Ukai ("It came. Small. Gone."). Ukai decides. Ukai still doesn't know there's an "eye"; he knows "looking too hard" and that Sho reports it.
5. **The gray zone is the drama.** A sting and a burn feel similar for the 1st second. Arc 3's real test is not "will he raise his hand at a burn" but "will he call a borderline one a burn when it costs him the set and they're winning". That's Satoshi's knee in small. Ch 38 is that moment.
6. **Practice counts.** The rule applies in the 3rd gym at night when no coach is there. Sho's version: stop, sit out, tell Ukai at breakfast. (A great small beat: Bokuto yelling "HEY HEY HEY where are you going" while Sho sits on the stage with his eyes shut.)
7. **Mio's log.** Recommend Mio keeps tally marks on the August calendar page under her magnet: 1 mark per "eye night" Sho calls her about. It turns "tell her all of it" into a visible object and a pudding ledger she can read at a glance.

### 1.7 Recovery and conditioning: the convergence work

The clinic said healthy eyes, exhausted convergence, "still a bit weak" on Wednesday. A follow-up before camp (ch 30 or 31, 1 short scene) can plausibly add doctor-prescribed exercises with home practice: **pencil push-ups and a Brock string** (a cord with 3 colored beads; you fix on each bead in turn). Real tools for convergence insufficiency; evidence favors office-supervised therapy with home practice over home-only, so keep the doctor in the loop ([Wikipedia: Convergence insufficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_insufficiency); [Brock string vs pencil push-ups](https://specialty.vision/article/brock-string-vs-pencil-push-ups-for-convergence-insufficiency-what-actually-works/); [What is a Brock String?](https://www.seevividly.com/info/Lazy_Eye_Treatments/Eye_Exercises/Brock_String)).

- **Mio holds the far end of the string**, 5 minutes a night, counts beads, and argues about whether he saw 2 strings or 1. Family texture plus a real training thread.
- **Effect, all arc 3:** burning recovery shortens from about 10 to about 5 to 6 minutes. **Thresholds don't move.** Not a card stat. It quietly builds the tolerance that makes Lv1's "about 3 clean reads per set" believable at Nationals.
- **Self-imposed screen budget:** the doctor's 1 hour a day ends at some point, but Sho keeps a version of it as his own rule ("Recovery is training", ch 6 plan, point 4, underlined twice). Consequence: **no film binges in arc 3.** He has to study players live, at camp. That forces Meta Vision growth and is the source of the marking weapon.

### 1.8 Does it fire more in arc 3?

**The rate per match stays about the same (roughly 1 played fire per match day). The volume of volleyball goes up about 5 times**, so there are more fires on the page in total, and they change character:

- **Early camp (scanning hard to build a map by volume):** fires are burns. This is the trap that leads to ch 38.
- **Late camp (quiet map running):** fires are stings, land at the right edge of the map, and the switch works.
- **Tremors: "embers" (new, late camp only).** The silence comes and 1 point lights on a **marked** player, with **no ghost**. It says *this one is about to act*, never *where*. Costs a faint warmth, less than a sting. 2 or 3 in arc 3, all on Bokuto or Hinata. Sho logs them: *Silence. No picture. Bokuto.* Their function: the 1st sign the eye is changing, and a temptation (he could try to blow on it; he doesn't). In arc 4 embers come more often (bible: "flickers come more often" vs Shiratorizawa). At Inarizaki the ember catches.

### 1.9 Can he influence it before Nationals?

Yes, 3 indirect levers. No direct one. "Never on command" holds.

| Lever | What it controls | What it can't do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Marking** (study a player for hours) | Raises the odds it fires on *that* player and cleans the ghost | Can't make it fire now |
| **Load** (quiet map vs narrated map) | Lowers the cost (sting instead of burn) | Can't make it fire at all |
| **Attention** (who he's locked on) | Chooses **who** it fires on, if it fires | Can't choose **when** |
| **Eyelids** | Can stop a fire forming | Mid-rally, useless |

Sho should work out levers 1 and 3 in the notebook during camp, in 1-line rules (*It likes people I've watched.* / *It only sees who I'm looking at.*). Lever 2 he understands only as a feeling ("when it's quiet in my head, it doesn't burn") until the final.

### 2.2 What each stage does in a rally (concrete)

**Sound layer, serve receive:** the server's contact. A float is struck with a stiff wrist and no spin; a topspin is brushed up and over [contact-sound difference: plausible, from memory; sources on the 2 serve types: [Wikipedia: Float serve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Float_serve), [Gold Medal Squared](https://www.goldmedalsquared.com/post/how-to-serve-a-volleyball)]. Sho hears a flat *pak* or a heavier wrapped *thup* and has his platform style chosen before his eyes have found the ball's spin. Then, after his own pass, he doesn't watch it go up: the sound tells him whether it's good, and his eyes are already on the far side 0.5 s early.

**Sound layer, own side in a rally:** Noya's almost-silent pass, Daichi's thud, Tanaka's flatter left arm, Kageyama's clean set vs a scrambled one (he hears whether Kageyama got under it), Asahi's last approach step (heavy) vs Tanaka's (quick). He covers before he sees.

**Wide gaze:** he parks his eyes on the far side's net center at setter height. In the periphery: who's front row, who's moving, who's on the floor after the dig. At the set he takes 1 last fixation on the hitter. It's 1 eye movement instead of 12, which is exactly why it's cheaper (author mechanics: fewer jumps and fewer focus changes for the convergence muscles; compare the sports-science idea of a single final "quiet eye" fixation [memory: Vickers' "quiet eye" research]; never name it in prose).

**Half map:** he knows, without words, where all 6 opponents are and which hitters are live at each set; where the empty floor is; where his block will be (by ear and periphery). It feeds weapons W3, W4, W6, W7.

### 2.4 Limits to show on the page (so the map isn't magic)

- **Transition chaos:** dig, scramble, overpass, joust: the map is stale for 1 or 2 touches. He's ordinary there.
- **Synchronized attack** (Nekoma, Fukurodani, and Karasuno's own new system later): 4 live hitters; the map shrugs; that's the moment the eye wants to fire (and in arc 5, the moment Inarizaki breaks him open).
- **The eye blacks it out:** when the eye fires, the far side is gray for half a second. The ears keep his own side. That's the switch (section 3).
- **Fatigue:** about 2 sets of full map, then words creep back. "Play all 3 sets" means learning to run a cheaper map in set 3, not a better one.
- **Noise:** the camp is the perfect lab because it's quiet. Spring High crowds will take the sound layer away in arc 4. Seed this: a loud Fukurodani bench or the adjacent court's whistle wipes it for a rally.

## 3. The interaction: why the map makes the eye "the extra"

### 3.1 Why a quiet map lowers the cost

Author mechanics, plausible and never lectured: the burn comes from the eyes working too hard at once: darting between 12 targets and refocusing at every distance, then the eye's hard lock on 1 player on top of that. A quiet map holds most of the court in memory and in the ears, and keeps the eyes parked. When the eye fires into a quiet visual system, it's 1 lock on rested muscles: a sting. When it fires into a scan, it's the 13th lock in 5 seconds: a burn.

There's a 2nd reason. A good map narrows the question. If the map says "it's Bokuto or the middle, nobody else", the eye only has to settle 1 of 2. It fires less often (the play outruns his brain less often) and the fires that do come land at the map's edge, where they're worth most.

Sho's version, 1 line in the notebook near the end of camp: *The map says which door. The eye says which hand opens it.*

### 3.2 The danger of fusing early (why ch 22 can't be repeated)

1. **Body:** convergence exhaustion, depth loss, nosebleed, and the MRI threat now hanging over the next one.
2. **Technique:** holding the eye inside a narrated map produces either a freeze (ch 14: 2 pictures, feet stop) or a hold (ch 22: 8 rallies on credit).
3. **Habit:** if he plays on the ghost, his body learns to wait for it. Noya's line from ch 9 ("you're waiting to be sure") becomes literal. Ch 22 at 21-21 already showed it: no ghost, misjudged a free ball.
4. **Tell:** when he holds, he goes still and looks at 1 player. Oikawa saw it. Kenma will. The more he fuses, the more readable he is.
5. **Team:** Ukai can't plan around something that comes and goes, and a player who might vanish mid-set with a nosebleed is a player you can't put on in a close set.
6. **Soul:** it's the knee. Said once already; arc 3 doesn't repeat the argument, it shows the alternative.

### 3.3 The switch ("Kai")

The arc 3 skill isn't fusion. It's the opposite: **when the eye comes, let the map go; when the eye goes, let it go too.**

1. **Cue:** the silence.
2. **Drop:** let the far-side picture go dark. Don't try to keep it (keeping it is what burns). Trust the ears to hold his own side.
3. **Take:** look only at the ghost. Act on it: move, call, dig.
4. **Release:** don't hold the afterglow. Blink.
5. **Rebuild:** on the next touch, by ear first ("Daichi's pass, clean"), then the parked gaze, then the map is back.

Sho's anime name: **"Kai."** Naruto's release sign for breaking a genjutsu (he thinks of it as letting the illusion go, which is funny because the eye isn't the illusion, his grip on it is).

**How he learns it without being able to call the eye:** he can't rehearse with the eye, so he rehearses the drop and the rebuild:
- **Blind receive:** eyes shut until the server's contact sound, then open: rebuild the court in under half a second. Builds the reboot.
- **Map-off rallies in the 3v3:** he deliberately looks only at 1 attacker for a whole rally, then rebuilds. Hinata finds this hilarious ("You're doing a staring contest!").
- **The real test** comes only when the eye fires on its own, in the Fukurodani final. 1 clean switch. Sting. No hand. That's the arc's payoff.

### 3.4 How this sets up the on-command awakening (arcs 4 to 5)

Arc 3 teaches him that the eye comes into **empty space**: when the play outruns the map and his words run out. At Inarizaki the twins' minus tempo and synchronized attack make the map useless every rally. For the 1st time he **drops the map on purpose**, the switch done without waiting for the silence, and the eye comes into the space he made. That's the full awakening: on command means *making room*, never *grabbing*. Ch 22 was grabbing. Arc 3 is learning to let go. Inarizaki is letting go on purpose.

## 4. Weapons

9 named techniques. **The names are Sho's private anime-brain labels.** Per the writing rules, 1 or 2 anime references per chapter at most: most weapons are never named on the page. Use each name once, at debut, as a single thought, or not at all. Out loud he only ever says plain words ("Two!", "It's the 4").

Success rates are author guides, to stop them from always working.

### W1. Suzumushi (receive by sound)
- **Name:** Tousen's zanpakuto in Bleach, the blind captain who fights by sound. Sho: *Bad omen. Keeping it.*
- **Stage:** Sound layer.
- **Cue:** the server's contact sound (float vs topspin); his own pass's sound.
- **What it does:** chooses his platform style at contact; frees his eyes from his own pass 0.5 s early so he can look at the far side.
- **Cost:** none physical. Needs quiet; learning each server takes a match or 2.
- **Counter:** noise (cheering sections, drums, the adjacent court); a server whose float and spin sound alike (seed for Miya Atsumu in arc 5: same toss, 2 serves); a float's wobble still beats him if his platform is wrong.
- **Rate:** improves his receive, doesn't make it great. That's Reception C, not B.
- **Debut:** ch 32, the 1st Tokyo match day, on a float from a Fukurodani or Ubugawa server.

### W2. Hiraishin mark (priming by study)
- **Name:** Minato's marked kunai in Naruto: the jutsu goes where the mark is.
- **Stage:** Film brain + Scanning. No eye control.
- **Cue:** none in a rally. It's preparation: an hour or more of live charting of 1 player (from the bench, in the 3rd gym, as the blocker facing him 100 times).
- **What it does:** the eye fires on that player more often and with a cleaner ghost. Bokuto becomes the arc 3 mark.
- **Cost:** time and attention. With no film (screen budget), only live study works. **Tunnel risk:** he over-watches the marked player and misses the unmarked hitter.
- **Counter:** players who change (Bokuto's moods flip his patterns); a setter who exploits the tunnel (Kenma sets the unmarked one); raw players who can't be marked (Lev).
- **Debut:** ch 36, 3rd gym. Named in the notebook as a rule: *It likes people I've watched.*

### W3. Kagemane (dig behind the block)
- **Name:** Shikamaru's Shadow Possession in Naruto (the strategist he'd most like to be). He stands in his block's shadow so the hitter's only open lane leads into him.
- **Stage:** Half map (a crude version works at Scanning, rarely).
- **Cue:** his own block's position (ear and periphery) + the hitter's approach angle and shoulder.
- **What it does:** he takes the lane the block leaves open, deep, and the hitter's best shot comes to him.
- **Cost:** commitment: if wrong, a whole lane is empty. Depends on an **honest block** from teammates, especially Tsukishima's read block.
- **Counter:** hitters who use the block (tools, wipes: Bokuto, Kuroo), tips, off-speed, deflections off the block (physics the eye can't read).
- **Rate:** about 1 in 3 against Bokuto. It's a numbers game.
- **Debut:** fails in ch 32 (Bokuto tools the block), works in ch 41. Tsukishima's reaction to being thanked for his block ("Your block was where it said it would be." "...That's what blocks do.") gives him a dry, small role without taking his moonrise.

### W4. Hawk-Eye (back-row calls)
- **Name:** Mihawk ("Hawk-Eye") in One Piece, and the tennis line-review system, both. Satoshi likes the double meaning.
- **Stage:** Scanning; better at Half map.
- **Cue:** the setter's hips and shoulders under the ball (dump tells), a hitter's approach slowing (tip), the back-row attacker's run.
- **Calls:** 1 word, from the **back row only**: "Two!" (setter dump), "Tip!", "Pipe!", "Back!" Front-row read blocking stays Tsukishima's and Kuroo's lesson.
- **Cost:** voice: opponents hear it and adjust after 2 or 3 calls (the ch 22 lesson about Seijoh hearing *send*). 1 wrong call costs the blockers a point and some trust.
- **Counter:** setters with no tells (Akaashi's even release); Kenma, who stops dumping the moment someone calls it and saves the dump for when Sho is front row.
- **Rate:** about 70% right at camp; falls when the opponent adapts.
- **Coexistence:** Noya calls serve receive; Daichi calls the team; Sho only calls the 2nd ball. Agree this with Daichi on the page in 2 lines.
- **Debut:** ch 33 vs Nekoma. 1st call is right; Kenma never dumps again that set. The adaptation is the point.

### W5. Kawarimi (the chin bait, and its 2nd layer)
- **Name:** the Substitution Jutsu: show them 1 thing, be somewhere else.
- **Stage:** none (craft). Already used in ch 22 against Iwaizumi and Oikawa.
- **Cue:** a good read blocker at the antenna who watches faces.
- **What it does:** look at the blocker's hand; the good ones move it; hit the opened line. Layer 2 (new): when they've learned it and leave the hand there (Iwaizumi's promise), tool the hand.
- **Cost:** works 1 or 2 times per blocker per match.
- **Counter:** blockers who don't read faces (Bokuto blocks on feel: immune, and funny), blockers who've seen it (Kuroo: "Not again." blocks him in ch 33).
- **Ownership:** "Face lies if you make it" is **Tanaka's** line now. In arc 3, Tanaka uses the face lie for a point at camp and Sho cheers. Sho's own version gets countered. That's healthy rivalry in 2 beats.
- **Debut in arc 3:** ch 33, failing against Kuroo.

### W6. Blind Spot (attack into the empty chair)
- **Name:** Neji's Byakugan blind spot. The joke doubles back: his own eye has one too.
- **Stage:** Half map.
- **Cue:** the map's "empty chair": the defender who just dug and is on the floor, the libero shading the other way, the back-row player late out of the block coverage.
- **What it does:** at the top of his jump he doesn't look for the hole; he already knows it and hits there. Defenders who read hitters' eyes get nothing.
- **Cost:** needs a set he can hit anywhere (Kageyama's flat-to-antenna, ch 22). Power C limits it: a soft shot into an empty chair can still be run down. If he ignores the block to aim, he gets stuffed.
- **Counter:** elite floor defense. Nekoma's (Yaku) barely has empty chairs: "Nekoma doesn't have empty chairs." Fukurodani's recovers fast (Komi, Sarukui).
- **Rate:** about 1 per match.
- **Debut:** ch 39, the 3v3 (3 defenders, the chairs are obvious). Once in the final.

### W7. Sogeking (serve targeting)
- **Name:** Usopp's sniper alias in One Piece.
- **Stage:** Scanning + Sound layer for picking targets; Half map for live seams.
- **Targets:** the receiver whose platform "sounds bad" today; a player just subbed in; the seam the libero leaves when he shades; short at the attacking middle to slow the quick; the hitter who's about to attack (make the attacker pass).
- **Cost:** **Serve D.** He can choose the target; he can only hit it about 1 in 2. A missed pinch serve is a free point.
- **Counter:** libero coverage (Yaku covers nearly everything), receive formations that hide the weak passer.
- **Ownership:** his toss is Yamaguchi's; Asahi's jump serve is Asahi's. Sho never coaches either of them on targets.
- **Debut:** a pinch serve in a camp match (ch 32 or 38).

### W8. Den Den Mushi (the line to the coach)
- **Name:** the One Piece snail phones: information only matters if it gets to the person who decides.
- **Stage:** any.
- **Format:** at a timeout or set break, **3 lines max**: 1 about them, 1 about us, 1 ask. Plus 2 signals: hand flat at the shoulder (burning, the rule), and a new one, a touch to the eyebrow from the court or bench ("I have something"), so Ukai can wave him over at the next dead ball instead of Sho shouting it across the gym.
- **Cost:** Ukai's attention; credibility (he has to be right); visibility (other benches see a 1st year talking to the coach every timeout).
- **Counter:** opposing coaches adjust at their own timeouts (Nekomata fastest).
- **Debut:** ch 32. Ukai: "3 lines." This is "Tell Coach 1st" turned into a tool, and it keeps Ukai the decider.

### W9. Kai (the switch)
- See 3.3. **Stage:** Half map + the partial eye firing on its own. **Cue:** the silence. **Cost:** a sting with a quiet map, a burn with a narrated one. **Counter:** opponents don't know it exists, but **attention bait** (Kenma) makes the eye fire on the wrong man, and then the switch only switches to nothing. **Debut:** ch 41, 1 clean use in the Fukurodani final.

### Not a weapon: embers
Late-camp tremor (1.8). Never used. Logged.

## 9. Pitfalls and guardrails

### Power creep
- **Hard caps for arc 3:** the eye stays at 0.2 s, 1 player, never on command; at most 1 burn on the page; about 1 played fire per match day; the map tops out at **Half map**; 2 status cards.
- **Weapons fail often.** Each has a named counter, and each counter shows up in arc 3 (Kuroo vs Kawarimi, Kenma vs Hawk-Eye and the eye, Yaku and Nekoma's defense vs Blind Spot, Bokuto's tools vs Kagemane, Serve D vs Sogeking).
- **Canon losses stand.** Karasuno loses to Fukurodani and Nekoma as canon. Sho's best day still loses 25-23. That proves the weapons are small.
- **Every gain has a cost:** a set lost to the hand, a mark that tunnels his vision, a call the opponent hears.

### Canon characters as props
- **Hinata's eyes-open quick is the danger zone.** Sho is the one character who knows what seeing too much costs. He must **not** solve it, coach it, or use the eye to help the new quick. 1 conversation at most, thematic only: Hinata: "What do you see when you're early?" Sho: "Too much." Hinata wants to see more; Sho needs to see less. Mirror, no help.
- **Ikkei's tempo lessons are Hinata's.** Sho's Ikkei scene is a separate, short conversation about looking.
- **Tsukishima's read-block lesson is his and Kuroo's.** Sho's calls are back-row only; his block lesson from Kuroo is the opposite advice.
- **Asahi's jump serve, Yamaguchi's float, Tanaka's face lie** stay theirs. Sho practices beside, cheers, and steals nothing he hasn't been offered.
- **Opponents stay smart.** Kenma's adaptation, Kuroo's tests, Akaashi's neutrality and Yaku's coverage must beat Sho on the page at least once each. Nobody gets dumber so Sho looks good.

### Explaining too much
- No lectures on convergence, saccades or "quiet eye". The doctor already gave the 1 medical explanation (ch 25, 28). The prose shows: eyes parked vs eyes jumping; the list in his head (scanning) vs no words (map).
- New ideas arrive as **a rally told in order first**, then at most a 1-line notebook rule after. Example rules: *It only sees who I'm looking at.* / *2 stings = 1 burn.* / *The map says which door. The eye says which hand opens it.*
- Weapon names: 1 per chapter at most, as a thought, never out loud.
- Ikkei: 2 or 3 lines on looking, no theory.
- Readers already know the cost order (burning, pressure, depth). Don't recite it; show where Sho is on it.

### Secrecy
- Nobody names it; nobody sees the ghost; Sho never tells anyone the picture, even Mio (she gets "what it was like, how long, where it hurt", in kid-level words: "Like everything went quiet and someone lit up").
- Max 1 near-miss conversation per chapter.
- The camp is full of readers (Kenma, Kuroo, Akaashi, Yaku). Let each notice a different *piece*: the stillness, the outcomes, the arm-watching, the early feet. Nobody puts the pieces together in arc 3.
