Stats in force: Power C, Jump B, Speed C, Stamina B, Reception D, Serve D, Block E, Set E, Mentality B. Eye: partial awakening (fires on its own, 1 player, about 0.2s). Meta Vision: Scanning (neck checks, snapshots, overload/freeze), aiming at Field map Lv1 by Spring High prelims.

## 0. The rules of reading (what makes this realistic)

These are the facts the whole plan should stand on. They're what keep Sho's reads from turning into superpowers on the page.

1. **Reading turns time into position. Technique turns position into quality.** Coaches and US national team players say at least 80% of serve-receive success happens before the ball crosses the net [S1]. Reading gives Sho that 80%. His D reception decides the other 20%, so his passes are early and on balance but not yet clean. That's the honest arc: "always there, not always good."
2. **Left/right is easier to read than depth** [S1][S2][S17]. That's a weakness Sho already has on the page: at 21-21 vs Seijoh he misjudged a short free ball at the 3 m line, and the eye costs him depth perception. Depth should stay his blind spot all through Arc 3.
3. **The expert edge is early body cues.** In studies of expert setters/blockers vs intermediates, the experts used kinematic cues about 60 ms before contact; intermediates waited for ball flight [S3]. Experts also show neural "simulation" activity 100 to 300 ms before contact [S4]. That's almost exactly the eye's window. The Emperor's Eye is a supernatural version of a real thing.
4. **Readers can be fooled by patterns.** In a volleyball occlusion study, viewers expected an attack pattern to continue and gave the hitter's body cues less weight [S5]. This is the most important tactical fact for the plan. It cuts both ways:
   - Sho's Meta Vision (pattern map) can be beaten by an opponent who deliberately builds a pattern and breaks it (Kenma, Akaashi, Oikawa).
   - Sho can beat readers the same way: build a pattern for 3 rallies, break it on the 4th at a big point.
5. **Head fakes really do slow observers** (basketball research: slower, more error-prone responses) [S6]. Good coaching says to approach the same way for line and cross and turn the head away from the hit [S7]. Experienced blockers counter it by watching the hitting arm, not the face [S8]. So Sho's ch 22 chin bait is real, and it has a real counter. Iwaizumi already named it: "Next time you look at my hand, I'll leave it there."
6. **Timing budget for the 0.2s eye** [estimate, rough physics]:
   - Hard high-school spike, about 80 km/h (22 m/s), to a back-row digger 6 to 7 m away: about 0.3s. A 0.2s ghost almost doubles the defender's window. **The eye is strongest on defense.**
   - Big jump serve, about 100 km/h with drag, over 16 to 18 m: about 0.7s. 0.2s is a big bonus, as on Oikawa's serve.
   - Float serve, 50 to 60 km/h: about 1.1 to 1.3s. The danger is the late wobble of the ball, and **the eye watches players, not balls**, so it adds almost nothing against a float. That's a useful built-in limit.
   - Blocking: 0.2s on the setter's body is like a commit blocker's timing with a read blocker's accuracy.
   - Attacking: 0.2s on a blocker's hands, read while Sho is already in the air, is only enough to change his **wrist and hand** (tool, roll, tip, angle), not his approach. Good. That keeps it small.
7. **Map vs eye, in one line each.**
   - **Meta Vision** tells him what's *likely* (tendencies, rotations, who's weak, where the gaps are). It works before the play and between rallies, it's on command, and in Arc 3 it costs mental overload, not his eyes.
   - **Emperor's Eye** tells him what *this one player is doing right now*, 0.2s early. It's involuntary, it costs his eyes, and per the bible it can't run with the field map (the forced fusion in ch 22 was the one exception, and it was paid for).
   - **Planning rule for Arc 3:** every signature play should work **without the eye**. The eye is the extra 10%, and each time it fires it's a hand-up test, not a highlight.

## 1. Serve receive

### What good receivers actually read [S1][S2][S9][S10][S17]
- **Before the serve:** server identity and tendencies, where the server stands (wide or middle, deep or close), where their feet and shoulders point. The passer has a plan: feet, track, forward [S1].
- **Toss:** a toss placed behind the server tends to produce a shallow serve [S1]. Toss height separates a jump serve, a jump float and a standing float.
- **Arm and hand:** watch the server's hand at contact, not just the ball [S1]. Arm speed tells short vs deep [S17]. Where the hand points after contact tells direction [S1].
- **Contact type:** a stopped palm with no wrist means float; a wrist snap means topspin [general coaching, memory].
- **Seams:** the gap between 2 passers. Teams set rules for it: the best passer has right of way, the player moving right takes the short seam, the player moving left takes the deep one [S11][S12]. Calling "Mine" is a separate skill from calling for a set [S13].

### Sho's reality
Reading makes his D pass *playable*: he gets there early, stops, and squares his platform. What breaks is the angle of his platform on hard or wobbling balls. The ch 22 run shows the edge (passed Oikawa with the eye, passed Iwaizumi off chest and arms without it). Arc 3's growth is Reception D to C, through libero reps with Noya and Yaku. Serve receive is also where opponents will **target him**: Seijoh served the pass on purpose ("every server attacks the pass"), and Nekoma's servers will do the same once Kenma marks him.

- **Eye adds:** on big jump servers only (Oikawa, Atsumu later), the arm's ghost shows the line (ch 22: "aimed at Sho's right hip"). It's useless on floats because it reads bodies, not balls. Good limit: Yamaguchi-style floats and Nekoma's float servers beat him at camp, and the eye can't save him.
- **Map adds:** server-by-server targets ("No. 5 serves zone 1 first after every timeout"), rotation prediction (who'll serve whom), and spotting when the opponent switches targets onto him. It also adds the **sound read** (ch 27-28): he hears a teammate's shank ("forearm, off the wrist") and starts the cover before he sees it.

## 2. Defense / dig

### What good defenders actually read [S8][S14][S15][S16][S18][S19]
- **Order of reading:** the set first (the set location decides most attacks [S15]), then the hitter's approach angle, then shoulders and arm.
  - An approach from outside the court tends to go cross. An approach starting inside the sideline near the 3 m line tends to go line or middle [S15].
  - Hitters tend to hit where their shoulders face [S16].
- **Eye sequence for a defender:** ball, blockers, ball, hitter (single block) [S18]. The defender is in the "second line": the block stands between him and the spiker.
- **Block shadow:** the area behind the block where a hard-driven ball can't land. If you're in it, move into an open lane or take the tip [S14][S19].
- **Off-speed tells:** a dropped "shot put" elbow; a hand you can *see* (meaning the arm is slow); a slowed or shortened approach; a set behind the hitter [S8][S20]. A straight arm suggests off-speed, a bent elbow a roll [S21].
- **"Posting up"** isn't a standard term in the sources. The underlying principle is: **be stopped and balanced at the hitter's contact**, square to the approach, in your lane, out of the shadow [S19]. I'd use "be stopped at contact" (Ukai's or Noya's phrase) as the coaching line.

### Sho's reality
This is where reading pays most and technique matters least. A dig only has to go *up*, not to a target. He has Jump B but Speed C, so he can't react out of a bad read (bible). Every good dig must come from being in the lane early, never from a dive he couldn't otherwise make. In Karasuno's base defense Sho plays left back or middle back when Noya's in for a middle [memory; rotation-dependent].

### Signature plays

| Rank | Play | Cue | Action | Counter | Technique / arc |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lane Post** | Set location, then approach angle, then where his own block is | Steps out of the block shadow into the open lane and is stopped at contact; digs it up the middle | Hitter wipes the block (ball goes where the shadow "should" be safe); hitter hits through a gap in a mis-closed block | Defensive base only. **Arc 3 core.** Pair with Noya: "Don't stand in the block's shade" |
| 2 | **"Feint!" call and pounce** | Dropped elbow, visible hand, slowed approach, set too far behind the hitter | Shouts the tip call (Japanese teams call a tip *feinto* [memory]; ch 22 used "TIP!") and moves forward, or lets the tip-cover player take it | Full swing that rolls deep over his head once he starts creeping; a hitter who fakes the slowdown and then swings | None. **Arc 3.** Counter beat: Bokuto's off-speed "is" a real swing until the last moment [memory: Bokuto has a deceptive feint]; Akaashi sees Sho creeping and calls the deep shot |
| 3 | **Tendency Trap** | Map: this hitter's ratio of line to cross by rotation or mood (film brain over 2 days of camp) | Pre-positions on the hitter's favorite lane before the set even starts | Setter changes the set (tighter, wider) to change the shot; hitter deliberately goes against type at a big point (Loffing pattern trap [S5]) | None. **Arc 3 late** (needs volume: camp is perfect). It's the payoff of Meta Vision's growth |
| 4 | **Ghost Dig** | Eye: 1 hitter's arm ghost 0.2s early | Turns his platform or shoulder into the line *before* contact, digs a ball he'd have shanked | Can't be countered by the hitter (it's real info); the counter is the cost (hand up). Also a 2nd hitter he isn't watching | **Rare in Arc 3**, involuntary. Should be the trigger for the hand-up scene (ch 38), not a win button |

### Eye vs map
- **Eye:** biggest gain of any skill (see the timing budget). It's also the most tempting, so it fits the hand-up test.
- **Map:** where to stand before the set (rank 1 and 3), which hitter is live (counting front-row hitters), when a setter dump is on (setter front row).

## 3. Blocking

### What good blockers actually do [S8][S18][S22][S23][S24][S25][S26]
- **Read vs commit:**
  - A read blocker jumps after seeing the setter's choice.
  - A commit blocker jumps with a chosen attacker (usually the quick) before the set [S22].
- **Eye sequence:** ball (pass), setter, ball (off the hands), hitter [S22]. A variant: ball, hitters, setter, ball, hitter, with a wide view first that narrows as the pass nears the setter [S18].
- **Setter tells** [S23]:
  - Watch the setter's hands, not the release.
  - Setter behind the ball suggests an outside set.
  - Neutral position suggests middle or back.
  - A dropped arm suggests a dump.
  - Know whether the setter is front or back row.
- **Line up on the hitting arm**, not the body, and take away 1 of the hitter's 3 options (line, cross, wipe) [S8][S14].
- **Swing block:**
  - Higher jump and more penetration [S24].
  - But a shorter approach and more time in the air. That means less ability to adjust to misdirection, more mistiming, and erratic deflections [S25].
- **Soft block** (hands angled back, not over the net) keeps the ball alive against bigger hitters. A penetrating block "seals" [S26]. Indoors a block touch isn't a team contact [S26].

### Sho's reality
Block E, though 182 cm is solid for Karasuno: taller than Tanaka and Daichi [memory: canon heights about 177]. His problems are timing and hand shape, not reading. Oikawa already scouted a tell: *"when it goes, Sho squints before he jumps and blocks too early."* So the eye actually *hurts* his block timing. That's a great built-in flaw, and opponents who know it can exploit it with tempo changes. **In Arc 3 his blocking value is 80% voice and 20% hands.**

### Signature plays

| Rank | Play | Cue | Action | Counter | Technique / arc |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Calling the set** ("Right side! Back set! The 4!") | Setter's hands, hips, front/back row; pass quality (a bad pass removes the quick) | Calls the set or the hitter a beat after the setter's hands start, for Hinata, Tsukishima, Tanaka | Setter with identical hands (Kenma, Akaashi, Oikawa); fast tempo so the call is late; a *wrong* call pulls blockers off the real hitter. Credibility is the currency | None. **Arc 3 core**, and it builds Tsukishima's read-block thread *without* stealing it (Kuroo teaches Tsukishima the read block; Sho just talks) |
| 2 | **Lane Block** (channel to Noya) | Map: where Noya stands, which lane the hitter prefers | Doesn't try to stuff. Seals 1 lane (usually line) with firm hands so the ball goes where Noya waits | Hitter tools his weak outside hand; tips over him | Needs hands that don't fold. **Arc 3**, built in practice with Daichi and Noya (Daichi canon: defense is a team system) |
| 3 | **Soft block, "One touch!"** | Big hitter (Bokuto, Ubugawa's tall wings), Sho clearly beaten on height or timing | Soft blocks (palms back), calls "One touch!", and the ball goes up for transition | Wipe toward the antenna; hard flat shot off the top of his hands out of bounds | Low technique. **Arc 3.** Turns E into "useful" |
| 4 | **The Late Close** (bait block) | Eye: hitter's arm ghost; leaves the line visibly open, closes in the last 0.2s | Shows a gap and takes it away | Hitter doesn't look at hands (hits the arm's line) | **Not Arc 3.** This is the bible's Lv4 bait read. Mark it for pro era or a much later echo. Swing-block footwork is an Arc 4 growth target |

Note on squinting: he can fix it in Arc 3 by *not* watching the setter with the eye. He blocks on the map (hitter count, setter tells) and lets the eye stay shut. That's a perfect "build the map so I don't need the eye" moment.

### Eye vs map
- **Eye:** reading the setter's body 0.2s early would make him an elite read blocker. But in the bible that's Lv2 (2nd year), and in Arc 3 the eye's cost (squint, early jump) makes him a *worse* blocker. Keep it that way.
- **Map:** counting hitters, front/back row setter, the setter's habits in each rotation and out of system, who the setter trusts at 20+ [S27]. All the calls in rank 1 come from the map.

## 4. Attacking

### What good attackers actually read [S7][S8][S14][S28][S29][S30]
- **Feet to the ball, then jump straight up.** That gives the best view of the block for shot choice [S28]. On a tight set, close the feet and wipe off the hands into the antenna [S28].
- **Shot menu:** line, cross, wipe/tool, high off the hands, tip, roll. Practice in blocks of 10 each [S28]. The more shots, the harder to predict [S7].
- **Disguise:** same approach for line and cross. The head turns slightly away from the hit [S7]. **Self-tell to avoid:** slowing the approach before off-speed. Young players do this, and defenders read it [S20].
- **Back row:** a back-row player attacks from behind the 3 m line. The modern pipe is a fast set, and its timing with the setter is the hardest part [S29].
- **The outside hitter calls hitters and reads the defense** [S29]. Back-row teammates can tell the hitter how many blockers are up and whether line or angle is open [S27].

### Sho's reality
Jump B and Power C make him a **shot hitter, not a power hitter**, and that's exactly where reading pays. Satoshi's college-ace brain knows the shots. Sho's arm doesn't yet: the bible says "no volleyball arm swing yet". Big risk: his raw approach *slows* on off-speed shots, which a reader like Kenma or Akaashi would spot in 1 match. Already on the page: chin bait on Iwaizumi (line), tool on Oikawa's still hand, Kageyama asking him for his toss ("Flat. Out to the antenna. Like Date Tech.").

### Signature plays

| Rank | Play | Cue | Action | Counter | Technique / arc |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Shot into the hole** (tip/roll to the map's gap) | Pre-set scan: who's cheating deep, who covers tips, where the defense's open lane is | Off-speed to the empty spot (zone 4 short behind a small block, deep corner behind a creeping digger) | Rotational defense with a dedicated tip cover; defender reads his *slowed approach* [S20] | Low tech. **Arc 3 core.** Growth beat: a full approach into off-speed, so the tell disappears (Kenma or Yaku points it out) |
| 2 | **Tool the still hand** (wipe) | Outside blocker's outer hand stays still or turns in; tight set | Hits off the outer edge of the hand into the antenna or out | Blocker pulls the outside hand in or *drops it* at the last second; a block set inside so there's no hand to hit (Iwaizumi: "I'll leave it there") | Mid tech: needs a tight set and wrist control. **Arc 3 growth target.** Hinata's "use the block" lesson from Bokuto at camp is Hinata's [memory]. Let Sho learn it from Asahi, or "steal" it from watching after Hinata gets it |
| 3 | **Chin bait** | Blocker who watches faces (Iwaizumi did once) | Head and chin to cross, same approach, hits line (or reverse) | Experienced blockers watch the hitting arm [S8]; Nekoma (Kuroo's read block) ignores it after one look | Low tech, but **it burns**: each opponent bites once. Use it at most 1 time per opponent in Arc 3, and show it failing on its 2nd use vs Nekoma |
| 4 | **Pattern break** (Meta Vision used against readers) | His own record: he's hit cross 3 times in a row from this rotation | Lets the pattern build, then hits line or tips at a big point | Opponent who knows he's an analyst (Kenma) "reads the reader" and guards the break | Low tech, pure Satoshi. **Arc 3 late** (Fukurodani match). Backed by the Loffing pattern-bias finding [S5] |

### Eye vs map
- **Eye:** a 0.2s ghost of a blocker's hands, while Sho's already in the air, gives him a *late wrist change* (tool instead of line, roll instead of cross). It's small, real, and costly. It also causes his "eyes half shut" hits (Oikawa: "Rude, but good").
- **Map:** shot selection before the set (rank 1); the weak blocker; which blocker jumps early on Hinata's decoy; his own pattern (rank 4); what to ask Kageyama for.

## 5. Serving

### What good servers actually read [S31][S32][S33][S34][S17]
- **Serve the lowest-rated passer**, but not if that passer is standing in your weakest zone [S33][S34]. Stats can mislead (a libero who looks worst on paper) [S35].
- **Seams beat zones.** Combine seam with depth (short seam, deep seam) [S32]. Kiraly: serve zone 2 or the middle to break the passer-setter link [S31].
- **Short then deep** to drag a passer forward and then push them back [S17]. Watch the receivers' stance: on their heels means serve short, crowding forward means serve deep.
- Targets can change a set faster than the score shows; track the opponent's adjustments [S34]. **Rule [memory]:** the server has 8 seconds after the whistle. Sho can use all 8 to watch the receivers settle.

### Sho's reality
Serve D. His jump float exists (ch 22, set point, built on Yamaguchi's toss, which Yamaguchi called "the best thing about my serve"). **Protected-moment warning:** Yamaguchi's float is his. In Arc 3, Sho's serving weapon should be **target selection, not serve type.** Keep him on a standing float or topspin when it counts, or a jump float that is visibly worse than Yamaguchi's. Serving is the **only skill where the eye adds nearly nothing**, so it's the safest weapon in an arc about not using the eye.

### Signature plays

| Rank | Play | Cue | Action | Counter | Technique / arc |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Serve the shake** | Map: who passed worst last rotation, who just got subbed in, who just made an error | Serves that player, every time, until they adjust | Libero shifts to cover them; team drops to 2 passers [S36]; the weak passer moves to Sho's weak zone [S33] | Needs only "in". **Arc 3 core** |
| 2 | **Serve the hitter** | Map: their best attacker also passes (Bokuto passes? [memory: Fukurodani's wing spikers receive]) | Serves the ace so he passes and then has to transition to hit, tiring or rushing him | Ace is moved out of receive; libero covers him | Low tech. **Arc 3** (vs Fukurodani). Classic analyst move |
| 3 | **Seam float** | Neck check: 2 passers with a fuzzy seam (a new sub, libero and OH conflict zone [S37]) | Aims at the seam, short or deep | Passers set a right-of-way rule [S11]; libero widens | Needs Serve C accuracy. **Arc 3 growth target** (D to C) |
| 4 | **The 8-second read** | Receivers' stance: weight on heels, a passer leaning toward the seam, libero shading | Holds the ball and watches them settle, then serves into whichever way the weight leans | Receivers reset after the toss; quick passer feet | Needs Serve C. **Arc 3 late / Spring High** |

### Eye vs map
- **Eye:** near zero (it might show a passer's weight shift, but the serve is a closed skill and it doesn't need it). **Use this to show restraint:** he serves well with his eyes resting.
- **Map:** everything. Pass ratings in his head, rotation order, sub tracking, each receiver's adjustments.

## 6. Setting / 2nd ball / emergency set

### What the sources say [S38][S39][S40][S41]
- **Out of system:** the setter took the 1st ball, or the pass pulled them off the net. Someone else sets. Teams name a default emergency setter. Left-side sets are the most reachable when the setter is pulled away [S38][S39].
- **Train non-setter setting on purpose** [S40]. A "high ball outside" is the standard out-of-system set [general, memory].
- **Defensive response:** cinch in against a setter dump [S30].

### Sho's reality
Set E. Realistic plays are the **high, safe, forearm or overhead ball to the outside**, plus *knowing* when it's his job.

## 7. Communication (the cheapest and biggest weapon)

Silence leaves the defense blind. Blockers are the first line of talk [S42]. Use team serve time to point out the big hitter and the setter's spot, and say whether the setter is front or back row [S43]. Back-row players tell the hitter how many blockers are up and whether line or angle is open [S43]. Good scouting charts tendencies by rotation and out of system [S27][S44].

### Signature calls (in-rally)

| Call | When | Who it's for | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Setter front! 2 hitters! Watch the dump!" | Before the serve, when the opponent's setter rotates front (5-1) | Front row | Wrong count = trust lost |
| "Right side! Back set! The 4!" (ch 22) | As the setter's hands start | Blockers | Late on fast tempo |
| "Feint!" / "Tip!" | Dropped elbow, slowed approach | Tip cover, Noya | Over-calling makes diggers creep |
| "One touch!" | His soft block touched it | Diggers | None |
| "Short!" | Server's arm slows | Passers | Server disguises |
| "2 up, line's open!" | To his own hitter, from the back row | Asahi, Tanaka | Distracting if every rally |
| "Shank! Cover!" (sound read) | He hears a bad pass before seeing it | Coverage | Unique to Sho. Only after ch 27-28 |

### Between rallies (Meta Vision's real home in Arc 3)
- **Through Ukai, not around him** (bible: tell Coach 1st). One fact per timeout, in plain words, then Ukai checks it. Ch 22 shows the model: the spoken *send* tip came via Ukai and Suga.
- **To Kageyama:** "Their 1 jumps with Hinata's feet, not your hands." What they do with it is theirs (the eyes-open quick fight is protected).
- **To Hinata:** nothing tactical about the quick. Let Ikkei and Kageyama own that.
- **Risk to dramatize:** information overload. Teammates tune out a 1st year who talks every rally. Daichi as captain asks him to pick 1 thing.

### Counter
Opponents with smart setters (Kenma) go silent, use the same hands every time, and feed Sho a fake tendency for a set and a half (the Loffing trap [S5]). The scene writes itself: Sho's calls stop working, and he has to go back to the hitter's arm (basic reading) instead of the map.

## 8. Libero-style training value

- **Why:** reception and defense need volume of reads, and a libero's practice is nothing but reads. Outside hitters handle a large share of receive. The OH is where the ball goes most often off a bad pass [S13][S36]. A strong passer lets a team pass with fewer players [S36].
- **What Sho gets:** Reception D to C (the earned rank change). The "be stopped at contact" habit. Defensive lanes. Libero and OH conflict zones (who takes what in the seam) [S37]. **And Meta Vision volume**: every rep is a read logged, which is how Scanning becomes the Field map without the eye.
- **Who:** Noya (instinct; "Don't think, go." He'd hate the notebook). Yaku at camp (precision and positioning; the perfect teacher for an analyst, and a real rival for respect). Later Watari and Komori (bible).
- **Cost:** fewer attack reps. Sho's arm swing (Power C) lags. Good tension with Tanaka (the same rotation-sub minutes).
- **Story value:** libero work is where reading looks like *nothing*. A dig with no dive, because he was already there. Coaches notice; crowds don't. That fits "soul over spectacle".

## 9. Master ranking: Arc 3 vs later

**Arc 3 core** (earnable at camp, no eye required):
1. Calling the set and counting hitters
2. Early Feet receive
3. Lane Post dig
4. "Feint!" call
5. Serve the shake
6. Shot into the hole
7. Lane Block
8. Soft block "One touch!"
9. "Short!" call
10. "I'll set!" bump set
11. Serve the hitter

**Arc 3 growth targets** (the rank changes Arc 3 can earn):
- Reception D to C (libero reps with Noya and Yaku)
- Serve D to C (seam float, standing serve accuracy; not Yamaguchi's moment)
- Off-speed with a full approach (removes his tell)
- Tool the still hand as a practiced shot, not a fusion miracle
- Pattern break (Fukurodani)
- Tendency Trap (needs 2+ days of camp film)
- Meta Vision Scanning to the edge of Field map (bible ladder)

**Later arcs:**
- Pipe/back-row attack (Arc 4)
- Swing block and read-block footwork (Arc 4)
- Hand set to Asahi at D (Arc 4)
- The 8-second stance read (Spring High)
- Ghost Dig on command (Nationals, Lv1)
- Reading the setter's body with the eye to call sets (Lv2, 2nd year)
- Late Close bait block (Lv4, pro)

**One-per-opponent tricks** (they burn): chin bait, Open Door, pattern break.

## 10. Camp opponents as counters (where each tactic gets tested)

- **Nekoma (Kenma, Kuroo, Yaku):**
  - Kenma notices patterns and gives Sho false ones (Loffing trap).
  - Kuroo's read block ignores the chin bait.
  - Yaku out-passes him and becomes his receive mentor.
  - Ideal for the "your calls stopped working" scene and the bible's ch 33 line ("You look at everybody. It's tiring to watch.").
- **Fukurodani (Bokuto, Akaashi):**
  - Bokuto's moods make his tendencies unstable. The map fails on a hitter who doesn't know what he'll do.
  - Akaashi manages him and sees Sho reading.
  - Best stage for Serve the hitter, Tendency Trap, and pattern break in the final match.
  - Karasuno still loses (canon), but Sho's thread wins something smaller: he plays all 3 sets on the map.
- **Ubugawa:** tall blockers, so soft block and tool shots. Low cost tests early in camp [memory: Ubugawa has height].
- **Shinzen:** solid all-round. Good for receive volume.
