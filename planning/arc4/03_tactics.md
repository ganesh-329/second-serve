## 0. The 1-paragraph thesis

An all-rounder gets the team to the 3rd touch. An ace is the player the team gives the 3rd touch to when the 1st 2 went wrong, and who turns it into a point or at least into "not a point for them." Arc 3 gave Sho the 1st job. Arc 4's job is the 2nd, and the honest way to do it with Power C is the way real undersized or underpowered outside hitters do it: **feet to the ball, one look at the block on the last steps, a full arm swing every time, and a shot menu wide enough that the block can't sit on 1 answer.** His reading helps him choose; his body has to deliver. Power C to B is earned by the arm getting fast enough that the choice stops being "where can I put this" and becomes "where do I want this."

## 1. What separates an OH ace from an all-rounder (high school level)

### What the sources say
- **Out of system is the ace's real workload.** Out-of-system means the setter can't play the 2nd ball or has only 1 setting option; often the only hitter you can get a real swing to is the OH in zone 4 [web: https://coachingvb.com/what-does-out-of-system-mean-in-volleyball/ ; https://coachingvb.com/working-on-out-of-system-play/]. USA Volleyball's tips say an OH may get about 50% of sets where they want them and 50% high, low, wide or tight, so the imperfect set has to be trained [web: https://usavolleyball.org/resource/good-habits-that-can-lead-to-great-results/].
- **Getting wide and waiting longer.** Coaches stress getting wide for the out-of-system ball and waiting longer to start the approach [web: https://coachingvb.com/working-on-out-of-system-play/ ; https://www.avca.org/resource/out-of-system-drill-progressions-for-setters-and-attackers/].
- **Feet to the ball** is the difference maker off the net [web: https://coachesinsider.com/volleyball/the-out-of-system-offense-article/].
- **The hitter calls the system.** One drill uses "Red" (in system, run the play) vs "White" (ragged, high ball to me) [web: search snippet, coachingvb.com out-of-system material]. That's a real, tiny, on-the-page tool: Sho's call.
- **Efficiency is context-blind.** Attack efficiency ignores how hard the swings were; 2 hitters with the same number can be very different if 1 gets the bad sets [web: https://smartervolley.substack.com/p/adding-nuance-to-the-in-system-label ; https://coachingvb.com/looking-at-offensive-performance-by-set-and-pass-quality/]. In the 2021 men's FIVB season, teams still killed about 37% after a poor (R-) pass [web: search snippet attributed to a Smarter Volley sideout-by-pass-quality post, https://smartervolley.substack.com/p/sideout-by-pass-quality-pt3].

### The difference, in a table

| | All-rounder (Sho at the end of Arc 3) | Ace (what Arc 4 builds) |
|---|---|---|
| 1st job | Make the pass; be where the dig is | Make the pass AND be the outlet when the pass fails |
| Typical set | In-system, from Kageyama, his choice of tempo | Whatever's left: a bump set from Daichi off the 3 m line, a 2nd ball from Noya, a high ball to zone 4 from the back court |
| Shot thinking | "Where is the hole?" (Blind Spot, off-speed) | "What does a full swing beat here?" Hole only when the swing can't win |
| Error tolerance | Low; a good passer doesn't give points away | Accepts blocks and some errors as the cost of taking the ball nobody else can |
| Mood | Calm, reading | Has to want it ("1 more") |
| Stat that matters | Pass rating, digs | Kill % on out-of-system balls, error %, the swing after being blocked |

**Fic rule from this:** the moment Sho stops tipping the bad ball and swings at it is the moment he becomes an ace on the page. A tip on a bad ball at a big point is an all-rounder's answer. In Arc 4 he should be shown doing the all-rounder thing once (it works, or it gets dug, and Ukai says nothing), then later choosing the swing. The coaching source supports this: in high-ball drills some coaches forbid tips and rolls so the hitter has to really challenge the block [web: https://www.hudl.com/blog/plan-your-attack-hit-around-the-block].

## 2. Attacking against different block types

### Block systems, briefly
- **Read block:** blockers wait for the ball to leave the setter's hands, then move [web: https://www.avca.org/resource/understanding-blocking-systems/ ; FIVB glossary via search https://www.fivb.com/volleyball/the-game/]. Usually later but arrives in bigger numbers on the ball's side.
- **Commit block:** a blocker decides before the play to jump with a particular hitter (usually the quick) [web: FIVB glossary; https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/244].
- **Bunch:** all 3 blockers start near the middle and move out to the pins [web: https://www.goldmedalsquared.com/post/blocking-in-volleyball-technique-footwork-and-team-systems]. **Spread:** blockers start wide, near their hitters, so pins are covered early and the middle is thinner.
- **Seams:** a middle who arrives late tends to float out in the air to close the gap and crash into the outside blocker [web: https://volleyballvault.com/volleyball-middle-blocker-tips/]. The low seam is rarely there; high hands or tooling out is the usual answer [web: https://usavolleyball.org/resource/how-to-succeed-as-an-undersized-player/].

### What each block type gives an outside hitter

| Block | What's usually open for a 4 hitter | What's usually closed | Fic opponent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Read block, bunch** (Date Tech's Iron Wall [web: https://haikyuu.fandom.com/wiki/The_Iron_Wall_(Episode)]) | The pin set that's fast and wide: the middle arrives late, the seam between outside blocker and late middle; tooling the outside hand on a wide set | The slow high ball: a read block loves it because everybody gets there; the middle of the net | Date Tech (if met), Shiratorizawa's ordinary rallies when Tendou isn't up |
| **Commit / guess** (Tendou [memory; fan descriptions in web: https://sites.google.com/view/haikyuu-statistics/shiratorizawa-players/satori-tendo]) | Whatever he guessed away from. A commit blocker who jumps on the quick or on a guess leaves a late-closing seam or a single block on the other side | The shot he guessed. A guesser who studies a hitter's habit guesses the habit | Shiratorizawa (final) |
| **Spread** (most high school teams [estimate]) | The middle seam vs a pin attacker whose middle must travel far; a back-row pipe | The pins early: outside blockers are already there, line is closed early | Ougiminami, Kakugawa, Johzenji |
| **Swing block** (taller, athletic blockers) | Off-speed behind an over-committed swinging block; the tool off a block that is still drifting | Hard cross into a big, late-arriving set of hands | Seijoh's front row (Matsukawa, Kindaichi) [memory] |
| **Soft or late single block** (small teams) | Line or seam with a full swing | Very little; this is where his kill % should be high | Wakutani South, Ougiminami |

### The 6 shot tools (each one with a real cue)

1. **Tool / wipe-off.** On a tight set close your feet and wipe the outside hand toward the antenna; swing flat and straight ahead, not down [web: https://usavolleyball.org/resource/tips-for-tooling-the-block/]. Sho already did it to Kuroo (arc 1) and to Bokuto's little finger (ch 44). In Arc 4 it becomes a practiced shot with a count, not a surprise.
2. **High hands.** For an outside hitter facing a big block, the best bet is often high off the hands or over the block; coaches train reaching high with an elastic band at the net [web: https://www.hudl.com/blog/plan-your-attack-hit-around-the-block]. The aim point is the top of the blocker's fingers so the ball deflects deep out of bounds. Cue: "hands high, thumbs back" is a blocker cue in the USAV glossary [web: https://usavolleyball.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Junior-High-Glossary-Cue-Words.pdf]; the hitter's job is to hit above that thumb. **This is the Power C shot.** It needs reach and wrist more than raw speed.
3. **Roll shot.** Slower, precise; heel of the open hand under the ball, swing hard and high, aim flat to the back wall; use it only after other hits so it surprises; a hitter who rolls only in front of a triple block is easy to read [web: https://volleyball.ca/uploads/Development/Coach/Skills/One_page_desc./ADV_DEV/EN/Hitting_-_The_Roll_Shot_for_e_module.pdf ; https://canada.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/attackers-success-determined-by-variety-of-shots].
4. **The seam.** When the middle is late, the gap between 2 blockers. Real but rarer than fans think; the low seam is "almost never" there [web: https://usavolleyball.org/resource/how-to-succeed-as-an-undersized-player/]. Best when a decoy (Hinata) pulls the middle and a pin blocker has to close alone.
5. **Line vs cross from one approach.** Volleyball Canada's stated goal: one consistent approach to the net that allows both line and cross [web: https://volleyball.ca/uploads/Development/Coach/Skills/One_page_desc./DEV/English/Attacking_Basics_Sharp_Cross_Court_for_e_module_skill_description.pdf]. Blockers read the shoulder angle (angled = cross, square = line) and the set location (pin set tends to go line, short set goes cross) [web: https://dickssportinggoods.com/protips/sports-and-activities/volleyball/volleyball-defense-how-to-read-a-hitter-when-blocking ; https://usavolleyball.org/resource/staying-square-to-the-volleyball-net-while-blocking-five-steps/]. One player's article: for line, turn the shoulder or rotate the torso, not both, or the ball goes out [web: https://www.volleyball1on1.com/?p=4269]. Plant tells exist (foot plant direction) [web: search snippet, secondary PDF].
6. **Off-speed tip** (already his "Blind Spot"). The Arc 3 fix was a full approach into off-speed. Arc 4 adds a full **arm swing** into off-speed (the arm slows only after it's committed).

### The unreadable approach (the mirror of Kenma and Akaashi)
Sho was read in Tokyo by stillness, chin, ears and feet. As an attacker the same readers look at **approach angle, shoulder, last 2 steps' speed, the plant, and the arm** [web: Dick's and USAV above; arc3 report S7, S20: slowing before off-speed is a youth tell]. The plan:

| Tell | What a blocker sees | Sho's fix (trained, counted) |
|---|---|---|
| Approach angle | A straight-in approach screams line; a banana approach screams cross | 1 angle for all shots: start wide, come in on the same 45 degrees every time |
| Shoulders | Open shoulders = cross | Hit line with the shoulder turn only, keep the torso the same as for cross |
| Last 2 steps | Slower for off-speed | Same speed every time; tape and Yachi's camcorder |
| Eyes and chin | Looking at the target | Already a lie he knows (chin bait, burned vs Iwaizumi and Kuroo); now "eyes on the ball, the block in the edges" |
| Arm speed | Slowing the arm early before a roll or tip | Full arm, change at the wrist and the hand only |

**On-page marker:** Yachi films 20 swings in practice. Tsukishima (or Ennoshita) watches the tape and calls line or cross after the last step, before contact. Week 1: 14 of 20 right. Week 5: 11 of 20 (close to a coin flip, which is the goal). The caller should be someone with his own reason to do it: Tsukishima reads hitters for his block, so calling Sho's swings is practice for *him* and he says so. That keeps Tsukishima's blocking his (Sho never calls Tsukishima's blocks; here Tsukishima calls Sho's hits).

## 3. Vision at the top: where the eyes go

### Sources
- Keep eyes mostly on the ball, take 1 quick look at the defense on the last steps of the approach, keep the ball in peripheral view; looking too early gives stale information because defenders move late [web: https://www.betteratbeach.com/blog/drillsforspikingavolleyball].
- Peripheral vision lets the hitter see an open seam or line while the eyes stay on the ball; an undersized OH has to use the edges of the block [web: https://usavolleyball.org/resource/how-to-succeed-as-an-undersized-player/ ; https://www.active.com/volleyball/articles/attacker-vision-training-874837].
- Coaches disagree about how much hitters can really see: one view is that elite players keep their eyes on the set and only *sense* the blockers' movement [web: https://marklebedew.com/2013/09/05/eye-work-number-4-and-counting/ ; search snippet].
- Gold Medal Squared has a dedicated piece on outside hitter eye work [web: https://goldmedalsquared.com/post/outside-hitter-eyework, title only; fetch blocked].

### A realistic eye sequence for Sho [estimate, built from the above]
1. **Before the serve:** the map. Which blocker is in front of him this rotation, bunch or spread, who's the weak hand.
2. **Pass in the air:** the setter (or whoever will set). This is where **the eye is legal and useful** (it reads setters): a ghost of Kageyama's hips and hands, or of Daichi's platform on a bump set, tells him a blink early whether it's tight, high, or off the net. He starts his approach a hair earlier, which is worth more to an OH on a bad ball than anything at the top.
3. **Set in the air:** the ball. 1 snapshot of the block on the penultimate step: how many, where the middle is, whether the outside hand is turned in.
4. **At the top:** the ball, the block in the edges. Wrist and hand change only (as the arc3 report already set).

So the eye's attacking use in Arc 4 is **on his own setter**, which fits the author's rule exactly and gives him time, the one thing an OH on a bad ball needs.

## 4. The map and the eye: what they do and don't do for an attacker

| Tool | Helps the attacker | Doesn't help |
|---|---|---|
| **Map (rules options out)** | Pre-set shot choice. Which blocker is short, who jumps early on Hinata, which outside blocker turns his hand in, whether this team bunches, who covers tips, whose defense creeps up. Also his own pattern (he hit cross 3 times this rotation). | Anything that changes in the last 0.3s. A map can't see a hand turning |
| **Sound layer** (inside the map) | The opponent middle's shoes. A middle crossing over makes 2 or 3 squeaks; the 3rd squeak late means a late block, which means the seam | Loud gyms. In the Sendai City Gym final with Shiratorizawa's cheer squad, it's gone (honest limit) |
| **The eye (hitters and setters)** | His own setter's body (timing on bad balls); the opposing setter's body when he's blocking or digging, which sets up transition | The opposing block, by rule. And it never shows the ball after contact |

### Can the map read the block in Arc 4 without the eye? Yes, in 3 earned layers
1. **Scouting layer (between rallies, Arc 4 from the start).** Long-Term Strategic Scrutiny applied to blockers: for each opponent he writes 3 lines on the bench (example vs Seijoh: *Kindaichi goes with H on fast, reads plain* (already in the ch 21 notebook); *Matsukawa's outside hand turns in late*; *Iwaizumi leaves his hand if you look at it*). That's realistic: scouting reports for blocks are ordinary coaching work [web: https://usavolleyball.org/resource/opponent-analysis-and-scouting-reports/].
2. **Snapshot layer (in the approach, Field map Lv1).** When the Field map forms at Ougiminami, its 1st new trick is to put the block on the map from **1 look on the penultimate step**, the same look coaches teach. Before Lv1 the look gives him "2 blockers, middle late." After Lv1 it gives him "2 blockers, middle's left hand at the tape, the seam is 1 ball wide for about 1 more frame." That's the map doing what a good hitter's peripheral vision does, just more organized.
3. **Sound layer (in quiet gyms only).** The 3rd squeak. Earned from Tanaka's 5 a.m. sound reads; fails in the final's noise, which forces him to swing without it in the biggest match. Good drama, and true.

### Should the eye read a blocker in Arc 4? Recommend: 1 uninvited flicker, on Tendou only, in the final
The argument that makes it earned: the eye reads bodies that **act before the ball**. A read blocker reacts to the ball, so there's nothing to see early. A **guess blocker commits before the set**, which makes him move like a hitter: his body acts first. So Tendou is the 1 blocker in Miyagi the eye *could* catch, and it does so once, without being asked, late in the final. It shows Tendou already sliding line. Sho hits cross through the space. 1 point. He doesn't try to get it back the next rally, it doesn't come, and he notes it in *SHO 2*: *T. moves first. It saw him.* That plants Inarizaki (Full awakening Lv1 can target a blocker) without breaking the Arc 4 rule in spirit. If the author prefers the rule untouched, the same rally works with the map: Tendou's guesses follow the hitter's last 3 shots (Tendou studies habits [memory]), so Sho builds a pattern for 3 swings and breaks it on the 4th (pattern bias, arc3 S5 [web: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01834/pdf]).

## 5. The high out-of-system ball (the ace's real job)

### Technique, step by step [web: coachingvb out-of-system; AVCA Keller drills; Coaches Insider OOS article; estimate for the specifics]
1. **Call it.** "High! Here!" or Karasuno's word. The call tells the non-setter where to put it (high, off the net, toward zone 4). Sho has a reason to ask for a specific set: he already asked Kageyama for "higher, off the net, for the out-of-system swing" (bible).
2. **Get wide and wait.** Back off to outside the sideline, start late, because a high ball gives time; starting early on a high ball means you arrive and stand.
3. **Feet to the ball.** The set will be off the net. He must move his approach to the ball, not the ball to his approach. Short last step and a jump forward into it if it's deep.
4. **Full swing, aim high and deep.** Off the net, the realistic kill options are: high hands off the outside blocker, deep cross over a short block to zone 1 or 5 corner, line if the outside blocker sets inside. A ball hit from 2 m off the net can't go steeply down; it has to go deep.
5. **The safe answer** when nothing works: a hard, deep, high ball to zone 1 corner (makes their setter run and their transition bad). An ace who turns a bad ball into a bad attack for them has done his job.

### Realistic numbers [estimate, from the efficiency and OOS sources above]
- Elite men after a poor pass: about 37% kills [web: Smarter Volley snippet].
- A good high school OH on true high balls: kills 25 to 30%, errors (including blocked) 15 to 20%, efficiency about .100.
- Sho at the start of Arc 4 (Power C): kills about 2 in 10, errors 2 in 10, efficiency .000. By the final (Power B): 4 in 10 kills, 2 in 10 errors, efficiency .200. That's the shape of an ace being built, and it's still worse than Asahi.

### Drill that should be on the page
"**10 bad balls**": Ukai or Suga throws/bump-sets a ball from the back court, high, off the net, to zone 4. Against 2 blockers (Tsukishima and Hinata, or Tsukishima and Asahi). Sho counts kills, keeps-in-play, and errors on the whiteboard. Week 1: 2 kills, 5 in play, 3 errors. Week 4: 4, 4, 2. Asahi does the same drill next to him and gets 6. The coaching source on facing the 2 biggest blockers when they know the ball's coming [web: coachingvb snippet] is exactly this drill.

## 6. Back-row attack and transition

### Sources
- The modern pipe is a fast set from zone 6; timing with the setter is the hardest part [web: https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/effective-pipe-attack/].
- Back-row hitters start further back and take off behind the 3 m line [web: search snippet, back-row attack guide]. Step counts vary by coach: 2 steps for a pipe vs 3 or 4 for a front-row approach [web: https://volleyball1on1.com search snippet].
- Transition: after a dig, the OH must first get **off the net**, then **outside** [web: https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/transition-approach/]. After blocking left, turn into the court, 4 steps off the net starting with the right foot, then a 4-step approach [web: GMS snippet https://www.goldmedalsquared.com/post/importance-of-good-transition-footwork]. A low, fast dig forces a shortened transition [web: https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/transition].
- Drill: OH digs to the setter, setter sets a pipe to the OH [web: volleyball1on1 snippet].

### Why it's Sho's weapon
He lives in the back row (receiving OH), and an OH is only a front-row threat for 3 of 6 rotations. A pipe makes him a threat in all 6. It's also the attack that most uses his strengths: Jump B, reading his own setter's body, and the map telling him which blockers are committed to front-row hitters (Hinata's decoy pulls the middle and the pipe goes through the space). The arc3 report already ranked "pipe" as an Arc 4 growth target.

### Back-row attack options for Karasuno
- **Pipe (zone 6, quick-ish set between zones 3 and 4):** needs Kageyama. Timing work is Kageyama asking the hitter what he wants (canon trait), which keeps it Kageyama's.
- **D ball (zone 1, set to the right side back row):** a slower set, used when Sho is right back. Less useful for a right-handed hitter from zone 1 (the ball is on his wrong side) [memory].
- **High back-row out-of-system ball:** when the pass is bad and the front row is a mess, a high set to the back row zone 6 for a full swing. This is where the "bad ball" and "back row" pillars meet: the ace's back-row high ball.
- **Synchronized attack:** in canon Karasuno works on everyone approaching together (the synchronized attack [memory]) at this time. Sho's pipe fits inside that team project; it must not become *his* invention. He's 1 of the approachers.

### Transition for Sho (where he's weak)
He receives a lot, then has to attack. The trap is passing and staying on his heels. On the page: Ukai's 1 rule, "Pass. Then go." 3 steps off the line after the pass, then the approach. Marker: in practice scrimmage, "pass then hit" success, 3 of 10, then 6 of 10.

## 7. Serve receive as an OH who must then attack

- Reception C is enough to pass and still hit, if he passes **high** rather than fast. A high, slightly off pass gives him time to get to his approach; a fast perfect pass to Kageyama sometimes means he's late to his own attack.
- Opponents serve the passer they want to keep out of the attack. A real tactic: serve the OH in front of his approach lane so he must pass and then attack (or serve the seam between him and Noya) [web: https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/serving-to-the-seams/ ; arc3 S17]. Seijoh and Shiratorizawa will do this to him. Ushiwaka's serve and Semi's pinch serve will go at the new OH [memory for Semi's serve; estimate for target].
- **Seam rule on the team:** Noya has right of way; the player moving right takes the short seam [web: https://coachingvb.com/whose-ball-seam-responsibilities]. Easy on-page line from Noya: "Seam's mine. You hit."
- Stat: pass rating 0 to 3. A good high school passer averages about 2.0 [estimate]. Sho at C: about 1.9; he should be shown passing 2.0+ on floats and about 1.6 on big jump serves.

## 8. Jump float targeting (and a conflict in the bible)

### Sources
- Serving to the **seams** beats serving 1 of 6 zones [web: https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/serving-to-the-seams/].
- Jump float: higher contact, flat trajectory, zero spin, approach and toss placement; short serves pull passers forward, deep corners push them back [web: https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/how-to-teach-the-jump-float-serve/ ; https://coachesinsider.com/volleyball/jump-float-serve-with-jim-stone/ ; https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/jump-floater-serve-keeps-volleyball-opponents-on-their-heels].
- Deep and lateral targets carry more variability and risk [web: search snippet, Frontiers 2026 PDF].

### The conflict (flag for the author)
The bible's Noun KB lists **Yamaguchi as the "Jump float specialist"**, and his big Arc 4 moment is the floaters vs Seijoh (bible canon timeline). The ace path says Sho learns "Jump float in Arc 4, his own." On the page, those are the same serve. If Sho debuts a good jump float before or in the Seijoh match, he steals Yamaguchi's moment.

**Recommended fix:**
1. Sho trains the jump float in Arc 4 under Shimada **as Yamaguchi's training partner**: they serve at each other's pass. Sho's version is "flatter, deeper, less wobble" (an OH's float, hit harder and lower); Yamaguchi's is the one that dances. Shimada can say exactly that.
2. **Match debut after Seijoh**, in the final vs Shiratorizawa, once, from the end line, at a target (the seam between Ohira and the libero, or at Goshiki as he rotates into receive [memory: Goshiki receives as OH]).
3. Serve C to B comes from **target accuracy** (the counted marker: 7 of 10 to a named seam in practice), not from the serve being scary. Mio can count with chalk in the yard (a sheet on the garage door with 2 lines).
4. Never put Sho's jump float in the same set as Yamaguchi's.

## 9. How Ukai uses a 1st-year sub ace beside Asahi and Tanaka

### The rules that shape every sub (verify for Japanese HS)
- Under FIVB rules a starter may leave once per set and re-enter once, only to his own position; a sub enters once per set and can only be replaced by the same starter; 6 subs per team per set; libero changes don't count [web: forum quote of FIVB 15.6.1/15.6.2 and general summaries: https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/how-subbing-works/ ; https://betteratvolleyball.com/how-do-substitutions-work-in-volleyball/]. Injury allows an exceptional sub [web: same]. Japanese high school play follows JVA rules close to FIVB [unsure; I couldn't confirm Spring High's sub count or roster size in Japanese sources].
- **What this means for Sho (important for the outline):** in any 1 set, Sho can enter **once**, for **1** player, and only that player can bring him off. If he comes in for Asahi, he cannot also come in for Tanaka in that set. A writer must not have him sub in for Tanaka at 8-10 and for Asahi at 20-21 in the same set.

### The 5 ways Ukai can use him (with the cost of each)

| Use | How it works | When Ukai does it | Cost / whose moment it risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rotation sub for Tanaka** | In for Tanaka for 2 to 3 rotations; Tanaka returns to his own spot | The opponent's block has read Karasuno's rhythm; Sho's different angles reset it (bible table) | Tanaka's ego. Keep Tanaka's reaction generous and loud ("Don't be bad.") |
| **Back-row specialist for Asahi** | Sho in for Asahi when Asahi goes to the back row (at Asahi's serve loss or after he serves, Ukai's choice); passes 3 rotations; Asahi returns at the front | A strong server targets Asahi in receive, or Asahi needs a breather | Uses both of Asahi's set entries. Ordinary volleyball (bible says so). Never at a moment where Asahi's canon serve should happen |
| **Pinch server** | In to serve; out when Karasuno loses the serve | Sho's target serve lines up vs a known weak passer | Yamaguchi is the canon pinch server; both can't pinch for the same player in a set. Use rarely |
| **Pinch server who stays** | Pinch serve, then stays for the rally and the next rotation (as in the bible table) | Late set, Sho's serve plus his back-row defense | Same as above |
| **Double sub (6-2 style)** | When Kageyama goes front row, Sho in for Kageyama (a 3rd front-row hitter) and Suga in for a back-row hitter (to set) [web: GMS double-sub snippet https://goldmedalsquared.com/?p=7724] | Late in a set when Karasuno needs 3 front-row attackers | **High risk**: it takes Kageyama off and makes Suga the setter. ## 10. The stats an analyst keeps (and realistic numbers)

### Definitions
- **Attack efficiency (hitting %)** = (kills - errors) / total attempts [web: https://volleyballvault.com/good-hitting-percentage-in-volleyball/ ; https://goldmedalsquared.com/?p=7672 (title)]. Errors include hits out, into the net, and blocked for a point.
- **Kill %** = kills / attempts. **Error %** = errors / attempts. Reported separately [web: Hudl glossary https://www.hudl.com/blog/ai-volleyball-stats].
- **Kills off bad sets** = kills on out-of-system sets / out-of-system attempts (the ace's stat). Analysts now adjust by pass quality ("kill % over expected") [web: Smarter Volley; coachingvb set-and-pass-quality post].
- Others Sho would keep (he's an ex-V.League analyst, so he'd keep exactly these): tooled blocks, blocked count, swing after being blocked (kill or not), pass rating 0 to 3, serve: aces, errors, and the opponent's pass rating on his serves.

### Realistic numbers
- OH benchmark: .250 to .300 good, .300+ excellent [web: https://volleyballvault.com/good-hitting-percentage-in-volleyball/].
- High school samples: all-area outside hitters at .216 to .238 [web: https://www.standard.net/sports/high-school/2023/dec/16/2023-standard-examiner-all-area-volleyball-2nd-team/ ; https://www.si.com/high-school/arkansas/2023/10/13/arkansas-top-high-school-volleyball-players-meet-the-states-best-outside-hitters]. These are US girls' high school; Japanese boys' HS will differ [unsure]. Use them as shape, not truth.

## 11. Power C to B: how his attack grows, on the page

Power B should mean: **he can hit a hard ball through a 2-man high school block's hands, or deep off them, from a high bad set**, not just place it. The arc of it:

| Stage | When | What changes | Marker someone counts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The swing is fine, the speed isn't | Ch 46 to the 1st week | Weight room; rotational med-ball throws (power from hips and trunk); band external rotation; "technique 1st" | Med-ball throw distance against the wall (Takeda's tape measure); squat numbers in *SHO 2* |
| 2. Reach | Weeks 1 to 4 | Approach work, penultimate step, arm drive | Mio's chalk: 318, 320, 322 |
| 3. Shot menu with a count | Weeks 2 to 5 | 10 tools, 10 high hands, 10 rolls (blocks of 10 are a real coaching format [arc3 S28]) | Tooled 6 of 10 by week 5 |
| 4. Unreadable approach | Weeks 3 to 6 | Same 45 degrees, shoulder turn only for line | Tsukishima's calls on tape: 14/20 to 11/20 |
| 5. The bad ball | Weeks 2 to 8 and every match | "10 bad balls" drill | 2/10 to 4/10 kills |
| 6. The swing after a block | Wakutani and Seijoh | He gets stuffed and asks for the next one | "Swing after block" stat: kill on the next swing |
| 7. Power B | End of Arc 4 (after the final) | The swing goes through Ohira's hands or deep off Tendou's | Reach 325; OOS 36% across the prelims |

**Realism check on reach:** +7 cm of spike reach across about 8 to 10 weeks for a 15-year-old who is still growing, with a new strength and plyo program and a better approach, is plausible but on the high side [estimate; I couldn't confirm a study figure this session]. Part of it should come from technique (the penultimate step and arm drive), which is fast to improve, and part from 1 cm of height growth (Dad's pencil). Splitting it like that on the page keeps it honest.

**The knee moment fits stage 2:** a sore left knee after depth jumps, told to Ukai before practice, depth jumps cut for a week, box jumps only. Reach stalls at 320 for 2 weeks (Mio writes *320* twice). Nothing dramatic happens; the stall is the evidence.

## 12. Concrete rally designs vs each prelim opponent

Notation: zones 1 to 6 (1 right back, 2 right front, 3 middle front, 4 left front, 5 left back, 6 middle back). Sho is a right-handed OH. Scores are suggestions and must be checked against each canon score (bible canon timeline) and rotation logic by the continuity agent.

### A. Ougiminami (spread, soft, small block) : Field map Lv1 forms
**Rally A1, "The map puts the block on it."** Ougiminami jump float to zone 5 seam between Sho and Noya. Noya: "Mine!" Pass high to Kageyama at the 3 m line. Kageyama sets a 4 to Sho, slightly inside. On the penultimate step Sho takes the 1 look: their middle is still at the 3 crossing over for Hinata; the outside blocker sets line. For the 1st time the look *stays*: block, defense, the empty deep 1 corner, all on 1 page. He hits seam, hard, between the outside blocker and the late middle. Kill. Why: a spread middle has the longest way to travel; the map gives him the seam the late middle can't close. This is the Field map's debut as an *attacker's* tool, which makes the forming of the map an ace beat.
**Rally A2, the same approach, line.** Next time in the same rotation, the middle arrives (they adjusted). Sho shows the same approach and turns only his shoulder: line, past the outside blocker's hand. That's the 1st "same approach" proof in a match.

### B. Kakugawa (Hyakuzawa, 201 cm, a tall block on 1 spot; a 1-weapon team [memory])
Hinata vs Hyakuzawa is Hinata's canon matchup (keep it his). Sho's angle is **the attacker vs a tall block**, not the hitter-vs-hitter.
**Rally B1, stuffed.** Sho in Tanaka's spot, front row zone 4. Pass from Daichi. Kageyama's 4 set is good. Hyakuzawa is the right-side blocker in front of him [estimate rotation]. Sho swings cross; the hands are higher than he's ever hit into in a match (Bokuto at camp was higher). Stuffed. 1 point to Kakugawa.
**Rally B2, high hands.** 3 rallies later, similar set. He aims at the tops of the fingers, flat, toward zone 1 out of bounds: the high-hands shot. Off Hyakuzawa's fingertips and into the stands. Why it's realistic: a tall, newer blocker reaches high but doesn't press his hands over the net or turn the outside hand in; high hands is the coached answer to a big block [web: Hudl]. It's the 1st time he chooses *off* the block as a weapon, which is Power C with brains.

### C. Johzenji (Terushima; flashy, flexible, fun; Kageyama's nosebleed, Narita in [canon timeline])
Not Sho's match. 1 rally is enough.
**Rally C1, the back-row pipe, attempted.** Johzenji's block is all over the place: they chase hitters with big moves [memory: Johzenji's playful style]. Sho in back row (zone 6 for Asahi). Pass from Noya. Kageyama's 1st pipe to Sho in a real match: Sho is a hair late, hits it into the middle blocker's arms, Johzenji digs it, Terushima scores. Kageyama to Sho after: "Earlier. ### D. Wakutani South (Nakashima, 173 cm, hits off blockers' hands; Nekoma-like defense; Daichi hurt, Ennoshita leads [bible])

Sho calls it ("High!"). Wide, waits, feet to the ball, full swing at the outside blocker's top hand toward the 1 corner: the high hands wipe goes out off the fingers. 20-19. Why it's the turn: he chose the swing on the same kind of ball he tipped in set 2. 1 point; Ennoshita's leadership and the match's ending stay Ennoshita's and the team's.

## 13. The traps

1. **Too good too fast.** If Sho's efficiency beats Asahi's at any point in Arc 4, it's power creep. Keep his overall efficiency under Asahi's every match. His win is the bad ball, not the kill count. Show him blocked at least once in every match he hits in.
2. **The eye on blockers.** Using the eye to read the block every rally breaks the author's rule and makes him unbeatable. Max 1 flicker, Tendou only (or none).
3. **Stealing Asahi's moments.** Asahi's jump serve, his "1 more" history, his big kills through the wall. Sho's "1 more" must not be in a rally where Asahi is front row and would get the ball in canon. Put Sho's ask where Asahi is in the back row or on the bench (back-row sub rotations).
4. **Stealing Tanaka's moments.** Tanaka's straight-line kills, his loud arc, his slump (Nationals). Sho in Tanaka's spot shouldn't happen at Tanaka's canon big point. Tanaka should get a moment where *he* answers Sho's minutes with a big hit (the rivalry makes Tanaka better, bible).
5. **Yamaguchi's jump float.** See Section 8.
6. **The synchronized attack and the pipe.** The team's tempo project and Kageyama's sets are theirs. Sho is 1 of the hitters who approaches; he doesn't design it, and he never gives notes on the quick.
7. **Substitution logic.** One entry per set per sub, only the same starter brings him off (Section 9). The continuity agent should check every sub.
8. **The analyst lecturing.** Rally designs explain themselves through action. Sho's thinking in prose: 1 look, 1 decision, no paragraphs of tactics at the top of a jump.
9. **Ace without the want.** Technique is half; the other half is Sho wanting the ball when it's ugly. If every kill is a smart placement, he's still an all-rounder. The tipped bad ball at Wakutani followed by the swing is the key structure.
10. **Montage.** Each gym or drill scene: 1 exercise, 1 number, 1 person counting (bible guardrail).
