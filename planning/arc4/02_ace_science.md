## Part 0. The short version (what the science lets the story do)

1. **Strength training at 15 is safe and expected**, with supervision, technique first and slow loading. The NSCA (2009) and AAP (2020) both say so, and the AAP report says well-designed programs show no harm to growth plates or linear growth. Sho's "technique 1st, light loads, write the numbers" is textbook.
2. **12 weeks of 3 x 40 min will make him a lot stronger and only a little higher.** Novice strength gains are big and fast (mostly the nervous system learning the lift). Jump gains are small: a few cm. The reach goal of **318 to 325 cm is plausible but at the top end**, and only if technique (penultimate step, arm swing) carries about half of it. Details and a safer curve in Part 4.
3. **The knee moment should be patellar tendon pain (jumper's knee) or its teenage cousin at the lower kneecap (Sinding-Larsen-Johansson).** It is the most common overuse problem in volleyball, it starts small, it is pinpoint, and it responds to a few days of cut jumping volume. Nothing dramatic happens when it is reported early. That is medically true.
4. **ACL prevention is mostly landing**: knees over toes (no valgus collapse), soft 2-foot landings, Nordic hamstring curls, a FIFA 11+ style warm-up. Satoshi's left ACL fits volleyball reality: right-handed hitters often land on the left leg after a drifting swing [memory].
5. **The shoulder is the 2nd ace injury**: build external rotation strength and scapular control, and count swings like a pitch count. There is no published swing limit for youth hitters, so Sho's count is his own rule [web, see Part 3].
6. **The jump float, the pipe and the high bad ball are all learnable in 3 months** to a "trust it in a match" level if practiced daily. Numbers in Part 9.

## Part 1. Youth strength training

### 1.1 Safety and the official position
- NSCA 2009 updated position statement (Faigenbaum, Kraemer et al., JSCR): a well-designed, supervised program is relatively safe for children and adolescents and can improve strength, power, motor skill and sports performance. Prospective studies show low injury risk. [web: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19620931/ ; https://dxpprod.nsca.com/globalassets/about/position-statements/position_stand_youth_resistance_training---2009.pdf]
- NSCA parent page: most reported injuries happened at home or with unsupervised youth; suggested ratio about 1 coach per 10 youth athletes, progressions suited to "training age". [web: https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/ptq/youth-performance-and-fitnessstrength-and-conditioning-information-for-parents/]
- AAP 2020 clinical report (Stricker et al., Pediatrics 145(6) e20201011): properly designed programs show no apparent negative effect on linear growth, growth plates or the cardiovascular system; use qualified supervision and proper technique. "Training age" (experience) matters alongside real age. Growth-plate trouble in youth comes far more from overuse and poor form than from lifting itself. [web: https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2020/american-academy-of-pediatrics-provides-guidance-on-resistance-training-for-children ; https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20200526/aap-kids-can-benefit-from-resistance-training-with-proper-supervision ; https://www.nike.com/hr/a/can-weightlifting-stunt-growth]

**Fiction use:** Takeda's library book is almost certainly saying exactly these things. Ukai's job, per the science, is to be "the qualified supervisor" in the room. Sho as the real expert and Ukai as the legally present adult is a funny, true dynamic.

### 1.2 What 12 weeks gains
- Behringer et al. 2010 (Pediatrics) meta-analysis: pooled effect size 1.12 for strength; gains were **larger with greater pubertal maturity** and with more sets and longer programs. [web: https://sponet.de/Record/4020373 ; https://painsmart-education.sydney.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Effects-of-resistance-training-in-children-and-adolescents-a-meta-analysis.pdf]
- Falk and Tenenbaum 1996 (younger children): 13 to 30% strength gains; adolescents and adults gain more in absolute terms. [web: https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol21/falk.htm]
- Obese-adolescent review: 2 to 3 sessions a week for about 12 weeks gives large strength changes. [web: https://bibliotecadigital.ipb.pt/entities/publication/d1527657-20e9-4593-9c4c-c3948729e89d/full]
- Novice lifters (any age) commonly gain 20 to 40% on a trained lift in 8 to 12 weeks, mostly from neural learning in the 1st 4 to 6 weeks; real muscle growth shows later [memory].
- **For Sho specifically:** 183 cm at 15, only 1 cm of growth from April to August (Dad's pencil: April mark 1 cm lower), means he is past his peak height velocity and slowing down [estimate from the bible]. Boys typically peak around 13.5 to 14 [memory]. Post-PHV is the best time in adolescence to respond to strength work (Behringer: more maturity, bigger gains). Good news for the story: his body is ready to respond.

### 1.3 Progressions and realistic numbers for a 15-year-old beginner
No youth norm tables exist for the goblet squat; adult beginner guides suggest a 6 to 12 kg start, chosen by a 10-rep test, "if 15 would be easy, go up a step"; dumbbells go up 2 to 2.5 kg, kettlebells 4 kg. [web: https://strengthlevel.com/strength-standards/goblet-squat/kg ; https://exercisepick.com/how-much-should-a-15-year-old-squat/] US national data (CDC NHANES 2012) used a modified pull-up (lying under a bar): boys 12 to 15 averaged about 10; about 15% of boys could not do 1. [web: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db139.htm] No strict pull-up table for 15-year-olds turned up; my estimate is 3 to 8 for an athletic boy [estimate].

Starting assumptions for Sho [estimate]: 68 kg body mass after camp (Mom: "You're thinner"), long levers (tall hitters struggle with squat depth and pull-ups at first), training age zero in this body, an expert brain. ### 1.4 A 40-minute session (Mon, Wed, Fri, 7 a.m.)
[estimate, built from the NSCA / FIFA 11+ structure]
- 0 to 8 min: warm-up (jog, skips, lateral shuffles, 6 Nordics in weeks 1 to 4 as part of warm-up, landing sticks, band walks). This is the FIFA 11+ shape.
- 8 to 30 min: 2 main lifts (Mon: front squat + pull-up; Wed: RDL + push-up; Fri: split squat + Nordic + light plyos).

## Part 2. ACL prevention and the knee

### 2.1 Mechanics
- Hewett et al. 2005 (prospective, 205 female athletes, 9 ACL ruptures): the injured knees landed about 8 degrees more in valgus (knee caving inward), with a 2.5 times larger knee abduction moment and 20% higher ground reaction force. Knee abduction moment predicted injury with 78% sensitivity, 73% specificity. Video analysis adds lateral trunk lean at injury. Small injury count; female sample. [web: https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/biomechanical-measures-of-neuromuscular-control-and-valgus-loading-of-the-knee-predict-anterior-cruciate-ligament-injury-risk-in-female-athletes-a-prospective-study/ ; https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/43/6/417.abstract ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4096083]
- Typical non-contact ACL mechanism: landing or decelerating with a straight-ish knee, knee caving in, trunk leaning sideways, often on 1 leg, often when tired [memory].
- In volleyball, right-handed attackers who drift or are off balance often land on the left leg 1st [memory; I could not verify a study in this search]. Satoshi's LEFT ACL is the natural injury for a right-handed outside hitter. The bible already uses this.

### 2.2 Programs
- FIFA 11+ and similar neuromuscular warm-ups: reviews find meaningful injury reductions, larger in female players for ACL; youth football studies report ACL risk reductions of 30 to 77%; adherence and coach buy-in decide whether it works. [web: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11899896/ ; https://journals.itb.ac.id/index.php/jskk/article/view/26363 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12856364/]
- PEP (Prevent injury and Enhance Performance, Santa Monica) is the women's soccer version with similar elements: warm-up, stretching, strength, plyometrics, agility [memory].
- Nordic hamstring exercise: van Dyk, Behan and Whiteley 2019 (BJSM, 8459 athletes): programs including Nordics cut hamstring injuries by up to 51% (risk ratio 0.49; 0.55 after removing high-bias studies). A later reappraisal says the isolated effect is inconclusive because Nordics were bundled with other exercises. [web: https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/21/1362 ; https://www.physio-network.com/research-reviews/thigh/why-methods-matter-in-a-meta-analysis-a-reappraisal-showed-inconclusive-injury-preventive-effect-of-nordic-hamstring-exercise/] Its ACL role is indirect: strong hamstrings resist the shin sliding forward on landing [memory].
- Standard Nordic ladder (the injury-prevention protocol): about 27 sessions over 10 weeks, from 1 session of 2 x 5 a week to 3 sessions of 3 sets, 12 then 10 then 8 reps. Mjolsnes 2003 (soccer, 10 weeks) went 2 x 6 to 3 x 8 to 3 x 12, then added load. [web: https://klokavskade.no/globalassets/publications/mjolsnes_2003_acsm_a-comparison-of-two-different-exercises-on-hamstring-strength-a-10-week-training-study ; https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/bitstream/11250/2390432/1/BahrBJSM2015.pdf] Nordics are brutally sore for the 1st 1 to 2 weeks (DOMS), the classic "I can't walk down the stairs" Thursday [memory]. In season, Sho should use the lower-volume end (2 x 5 to 3 x 6) because a full ladder makes practice legs heavy.
- Volleyball injury pattern: acute ankle sprains (often landing on a teammate's or opponent's foot under the net; the centerline is a listed risk factor) are the most common acute injury; then overuse of the knee (mostly patellar tendinopathy) and shoulder. Ankle sprains in matches happen at about twice the practice rate. One recent review: knee 30% of injuries, ankle 26%, fingers 22%, back 17%. [web: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2564299 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10234904/ ; https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/40/7/594]

**What Sho's landing checklist looks like** (cues coaches use) [memory]: land on both feet when you can; land soft, on the balls of the feet rolling to heels; knees bend and point over the toes; hips back; chest over knees; no sideways trunk lean; "quiet landing" (a loud landing is a stiff landing). Coaches film landings from the front: knees that touch or cave in on a drop jump are the red flag.

### 2.3 The knee moment: what early patellar tendon pain really looks like
**Facts:**
- Jumper's knee (patellar tendinopathy) is common in volleyball: 40 to 50% prevalence reported in elite players; most Korean adolescent elite players in one sample had patellar tendon problems. Diagnosis: pain in the patellar tendon during or after loading activity, with tenderness. VISA-P (0 to 100 questionnaire): healthy about 93, symptomatic adolescents about 68. Volleyball players who developed it in adolescence still had reduced knee function 11 years later. [web: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10482817/ ; https://synapse.koreamed.org/articles/1149718 ; https://www.e-arm.org/journal/view.php?number=322]
- In adolescent athletes, high patellar tendon strain (the muscle gets strong faster than the tendon stiffens) raised the risk of tendon pain 2.3 times. [web: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10482817/] This is the exact mechanism for a teen who starts lifting: **quads get strong in 6 weeks, tendon adapts over months**. The plan's knee moment should come right when the squat numbers and box height go up together.
- Risk factors: jump volume and training volume, sets played per week (limited and conflicting evidence); weekly training hours. Time on court is a poor proxy: jump counts vary a lot between players in the same practice. [web: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30054341/ ; https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/2602829/Bahr%2BScandJMedSciSport%2B2018.pdf?sequence=1 ; https://lermagazine.com/?p=7113]
- Teen cousins: **Sinding-Larsen-Johansson** (pain at the lower tip of the kneecap, the adolescent version of jumper's knee) and **Osgood-Schlatter** (pain and a bump at the top of the shin, below the tendon). Both cluster around the growth spurt (boys about 10 to 14), more in boys, common in volleyball, diagnosed by examination, managed by reducing (not stopping) painful activity; usually self-limiting. [web: https://www.fittoplay.org/body-parts/knee/osgood-schlatter-and-sinding-larsen-johansson-syndrome ; https://emedicine.com/sports/topic89.htm ; https://www.sfh-tr.nhs.uk/media/3768/osgood-schlatters-disease.pdf] At 15 and post-PHV, Sho is late for these, so adult-style patellar tendon pain at the lower pole is the cleanest choice [estimate].
- Classic staging (Blazina) [memory]: stage 1, pain only after activity; stage 2, pain at the start, fades once warm, comes back when tired; stage 3, pain during and after that hurts performance; stage 4, rupture. **Early = stage 1.** That is the stage Satoshi trained through for 3 months.
- Clinicians test it with a **single-leg decline squat** on a 25 degree board, rating pain 0 to 10. Common rule: keep loading if pain stays at or below about 3 to 5 out of 10 during and settles by the next morning (pain-monitoring model, Thomee; Silbernagel for Achilles). [web: https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/sites.udel.edu/dist/c/3448/files/2016/01/Patellar-Tendinopathy.pdf ; https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/sensitivity-to-change-and-responsiveness-of-provocative-load-test/] Isometric holds (wall sits, Spanish squat with a belt around a pole, 5 x 45 s) are often used to calm tendon pain in season [memory; evidence for their pain relief is mixed].

**How it feels, in words a 15-year-old would use** [memory, clinical descriptions]: a small spot, 1 fingertip wide, just below the kneecap; sore on the 1st stairs in the morning or going down stairs; stiff after sitting through a class; fine once warm in practice, then aching in the evening; tender if you press it. No swelling, no locking, no giving way.

**Red flags that would make it a doctor visit the same day** [memory]: swelling, a pop, the knee giving way, locking or catching, pain at night or at rest, pain that changes how he walks, a limp. None of those should happen in Arc 4. The point of the moment is that it is small.

**How the undramatic version goes (medically real):**
1. Wednesday, week 6: left knee, lower pole of the kneecap, 2 out of 10 on the stairs, 0 walking. Came after the week the front squat went up and the box went from 50 to 60 cm.
2. He tells Ukai before practice. Ukai presses it (or asks him to), has him do 1 single-leg decline squat on the stairs: 3 out of 10.
3. Plan: no plyos, no approach jumps, no jump float for 5 to 7 days; he still passes, digs, sets, hits from a standing jump at half volume or off a box; wall sits 5 x 45 s twice a day; lifts continue with lighter squats; ice if he wants. Report every morning (0 to 10).
4. Days 3 to 5: pain 1, then 0 on stairs. Back to approach jumps in a ladder over the next week (10, then 20, then full). Reach dips about 1 cm and comes back.
5. Total cost: about 1 week of reduced jumping, 1 measured reach that didn't go up. That is the real-world outcome for stage 1 reported early [estimate from clinical practice].

**Satoshi continuity (careful):** ACL tears are usually sudden, on a landing or a cut; 3 months of tendon pain doesn't itself tear an ACL. But pain and fatigue change how you land (you protect the sore side, land stiff or crooked), and that is the honest link [estimate]. The bible's version (trained through 3 months of pain, then the ACL went) works if the pain was the warning nobody heard, rather than the direct cause. Never explain it in prose.

## Part 3. Shoulder health for hitters
- Overhead-athlete review: scapular dyskinesis is a possible risk factor for chronic shoulder pain; strengthening and mobility programs are the prevention base. [web: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4647145/]
- Volleyball: preseason **weak external rotation and a low ER/IR strength ratio** in the hitting shoulder were found in players who later got shoulder injuries; the dominant-shoulder ER/IR ratio is low in male players regardless of level. Authors call for systematic external rotator strengthening, especially in men. Evidence is mixed overall (small, partly cross-sectional studies). [web: https://doi.org/10.3233/IES-210127 ; https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4080093 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6410282]
- About 40% of adolescent players report shoulder pain without injury; only a 3rd of them take time off. Overuse is under-counted because it rarely costs playing time. Spike ball-contact force changes shoulder torque sharply at contact. [web: https://www.jssm.org/jssm-22-488.xml-Fulltext ; https://www.jssm.org/volume22/iss3/XML/jssm-22-487.xml]
- **No validated swing count exists** for youth volleyball; load guidance is individual (USA Volleyball sports medicine commission). Baseball pitch counts are the model people point to. [web: https://coachesinsider.com/volleyball/does-playing-too-much-volleyball-lead-to-injury-article/ ; https://www.therapeuticassociates.com/youth-volleyball-injuries-prevention-and-treatment/]
- Shoulder program [memory]: band ER at 0 and 90 degrees, side-lying ER, prone Y-T-W, scapular push-ups, wall slides, thoracic rotation (open books), sleeper stretch only if internal rotation is short, rows and face pulls, pull-ups. 2 to 3 sets, every session.

**Sho's "pitch count"** [estimate, his own invented rule]: full-power swings (spikes plus jump serves) counted per day with tally marks; a soft ceiling around 80 to 100 full swings on a practice day, 120 on a match day with warm-up; off-speed and standing tips not counted. When the count is high 2 days in a row, the 3rd day is serving and passing. Asahi has never counted a swing in his life. That contrast is gold.

## Part 4. Jump, approach and reach

### 4.1 The approach
- The 4-step approach (right-hander: right, left, right-left; slow to fast) ends in a long, low **penultimate step** (the last right foot, for a right-hander) that converts run-up speed into vertical; the quads are loaded eccentrically on the last steps. Knee extension speed and step length predicted jump height in elite female players. [web: https://iris.unicas.it/bitstream/11580/91401/1/02640414.2019.pdf ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12751981/ ; https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/2606/2454]
- Approach jump vs standing jump: D1 women gained about 5 cm from a 4-step approach (38 vs 33 cm); another lab found 61 vs 48 cm; in coaching tests the numbers run even higher because of how they are measured. A typical approach adds roughly 5 to 13 cm over a standing countermovement jump. Approach speed matters in some studies (r = 0.71) and not in others. [web: https://lida.sport-iat.de/dvv/Record/4020473 ; https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4070277 ; https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/3163/2967]
- Arm swing: one source says the arm swing adds about 10 cm to jump height (citing Lees, Vanrenterghem and De Clercq 2004); a small elite-volleyball study put the arm swing's share at about 27%; in young children it adds 12 to 15%. Commonly quoted: about 10% of jump height [memory]. [web: https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/Determinants_of_countermovement_jump_performance_a_kinetic_and_kinematic_analysis/9617123/files/17264789.pdf ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12655968/]
- Common approach faults Sho would fix [memory]: a short, choppy penultimate; arms swinging late (the arms should be fully back as the penultimate lands, then thrown up together on the plant); jumping forward into the net (broad jump) instead of up; planting with the feet square to the net (you lose hip rotation); the left foot "stomps" late.

### 4.2 Reach numbers: plausibility check for 183 cm
- Standing reach (1 arm, feet flat) is usually about 1.31 to 1.35 times height [memory]. For 183 cm: **240 to 247 cm; use 242 cm** [estimate]. The men's net is 243 cm; Japanese high school boys play at 240 cm [memory; check with the canon agent]. So his standing reach is about the height of the net tape. A real, quiet page detail.
- Spike reach 318 cm means an approach jump touch of 318 minus 242 = **76 cm**. That is very good for a 15-year-old (equivalent to a standing jump-and-reach of about 63 to 70 cm, given the 5 to 13 cm approach bonus) [estimate]. It fits "Jump B" for a naturally springy, tall 1st year at a decent Miyagi school. Upper end, still believable.
- Japanese pro reference points (Wikipedia profiles): Asano 178 cm, spike 335; Fukatsu 180 cm, 330; Yoneyama 185 cm, 340; Watanabe 196 cm, 335. Profile reach numbers vary by source (1 player is listed at both 325 and 340). [web: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroaki_Asano ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hideomi_Fukatsu ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuta_Yoneyama ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogo_Watanabe ; https://volleyballvault.com/the-highest-vertical-jumps-in-volleyball/] **Satoshi's 340 in college at roughly the same height is plausible** for an elite college ace (pro-level jump). Sho at 335 in his 2nd year (with growth to about 185) is plausible too. A satisfying analyst detail: Sho knows profile numbers are rounded up, so he never trusts any number he didn't chalk himself.
- Japanese research: 最高到達点 (highest reach) is listed in player profiles next to name and height and used as a key ability indicator; 1 small high school club's team average rose from 237 to 264 cm over several years (small and unrepresentative). [web: https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/38485/files/%E7%94%B7%E5%AD%90%E3%83%90%E3%83%AC%E3%83%BC%E3%83%9C%E3%83%BC%E3%83%AB%E9%81%B8%E6%89%8B%E3%81%AE%E8%BA%AB%E9%95%B7%E3%81%AB%E9%96%A2%E3%81%99%E3%82%8B%E7%A0%94%E7%A9%B6.pdf ; https://iwate-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/13775/files/al-no31p095-115.pdf]

### 4.3 Realistic gains in about 12 weeks: is 318 to 325 plausible?
- Plyometric meta-analyses: large effects on jump in adolescents (63 RCTs, 10 to 19 years, effect 0.90 for volleyball players; best at 10 to 26 weeks); low-volume, low-frequency programs work for volleyball players; more total jumps did not consistently mean more gain. [web: https://peerj.com/reviewer-match/129815 ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32874101/ ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2026.1760239/full ; https://doaj.org/article/b0e7e288c5e34058bc7e436b13e22c49] Markovic 2007 (BJSM) reported about 5 to 9% countermovement jump gains from plyometric programs [memory], which for Sho's jump is about 3 to 5 cm.
- Where 7 cm could come from [estimate]:
  - Growth: about 1 cm of height by November (he is decelerating, about 3 cm a year) adds about 1.3 cm of reach.
  - Strength and plyos: 2 to 4 cm of jump in 12 weeks for a novice lifter, delayed (shows in weeks 6 to 12, not 1 to 4), interrupted by the knee week.
  - Technique: penultimate step and arm timing on an untrained-in-this-body approach: 2 to 3 cm. This comes fast (weeks 2 to 5).
  - Measurement learning: the 1st chalk marks will be low because he isn't used to reaching at the top (1 cm).
  - Total: about 5 to 9 cm. **325 by the prelims is plausible, at the optimistic end.** Safer: 323 or 324 at the prelims, 325 on a fresh day in the week of the final, as his best of 3. The page should show the curve flatten (weeks with 0 cm) and dip (knee week, a tired week before the tournament). A straight-line gain would feel fake to anyone who has trained.
- Important realism: in-season, after a 3-hour practice, nobody jumps their best. Real measurement days are Monday mornings after Sunday rest, or the week of a taper.

## Part 5. Arm swing biomechanics
- The spike is a proximal-to-distal kinetic chain: legs, hips, trunk, shoulder, elbow, wrist, fingers; segment speeds rise down the chain (1 study: shoulder 3.7 m/s, finger 17.3 m/s; mean ball 26.2 m/s, about 94 km/h, in good players). [web: https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/776/699]
- **Shoulder-hip separation angle** was the strongest predictor of cross-court spike speed in 14 D1 female players, ahead of shoulder and hip rotation speed. [web: https://core.ac.uk/works/121479538] (This is the analyst's favorite number: the hips turn 1st, the shoulders wait, then snap.)
- Back-row spikes: higher jump but lower ball speed than front-row spikes in one comparison, attributed to timing. [web: https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/1952/1820]
- Contact point: straight arm, highest reachable point, slightly in front of the hitting shoulder; "jump so the ball is in front of your hitting shoulder" (USA Volleyball). [web: https://usavolleyball.org/resource/how-can-i-spike-harder/ ; https://coachingvb.com/volleyball-arm-swing-understanding-coaching-it/]
- **The live debate:** bow and arrow with a high elbow is easy to teach, but some PT-informed coaches argue a lower-elbow load keeps the shoulder in a healthier position and that power should come from the trunk; 1 coach stopped teaching bow and arrow. [web: https://betteratbeach.com/blog/armswingforspikingavolleyball ; https://smartervolley.substack.com/p/fall-2025-mailbag-part-1 ; https://coachesinsider.com/volleyball/swing-mechanics-getting-into-the-swing-of-things-article] Fiction use: Satoshi was taught the high-elbow bow and arrow in 2010s Japan; the 2020s analyst in him knows the argument. He keeps the high elbow for the look of it (the bible says bow and arrow) but rotates more from the hips and lets the elbow drop a few cm on the load. Ukai notices the swing "looks different from Asahi's". Nobody explains.
- Typical ball speeds [estimate]: a good high school boy 70 to 85 km/h; Asahi-level 90+; pro men 100 to 120 km/h [memory]. Sho, week 1, about 75; week 12, about 80. Strength helps power a little; separation and timing help more.

## Part 6. The jump float serve
- Match data (Moras 2008, 377 serves at a high-level tournament): jump spin about 23 m/s; jump float about 12 m/s; standing float about 11.5 m/s. Standing float had the most negative outcomes (66.7%), jump float a better balance (42.9% negative). Speed did not predict effectiveness. [web: https://lida.sport-iat.de/dvv/Record/4014785]
- Floats are hard to receive and have fewer errors than the jump spin. [web: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2020.559277/pdf]
- Why the jump float works [memory]: higher contact point, so a flatter trajectory can clear the net and still drop; less time for the receiver; the float wobble still happens at about the same speed.
- Mechanics: approach like the attack (Jim Stone: 4 steps, toss on the 2nd), or a short 2 to 3 step version; toss low, without spin, in front of the hitting shoulder, leading into the court; firm hand and wrist, contact through the center of the ball (some coach the heel of the hand), short follow-through with the palm to the target; a downward swing or a wrist roll turns it into a weak topspin; learn it as "the same strike as the standing float, just higher". [web: https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/how-to-teach-the-jump-float-serve/ ; https://coachesinsider.com/volleyball/jump-float-serve-with-jim-stone/ ; https://usavolleyball.org/resource/five-keys-to-a-wicked-jump-float-serve/ ; https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/jump-floater-serve-keeps-volleyball-opponents-on-their-heels]
- How long to learn [estimate]: a player with a solid standing float (Sho, Serve C) can get the jump float into the court 6 to 7 times in 10 in practice within 2 to 3 weeks of daily reps (20 to 30 a day); reliable enough to use in a match (8 in 10, aimed at a zone) in 6 to 8 weeks. Pressure costs about 1 in 10.
- **Protected moment:** Yamaguchi owns Karasuno's float. Sho can ask him a question about the wrist (he asks, Yamaguchi answers, a small earned moment for Yamaguchi) but never teaches him. Asahi owns the jump spin.

## Part 7. Back-row attack (pipe and D)
- Rule: take-off behind the 3 m line; landing inside it is legal. [web: https://www.sportplan.net/drills/Volleyball/trends/back-row-attack-offensive-threat-2025-04.jsp]
- Volleyball Canada footwork: start about 3 m behind the 3 m line; the 2nd-last step lands about 1 m from the line; the last step plants as close to the line as possible; the gap lets the hitter drift forward and hit from inside the line. Right-handers: left, right, left. Common error: starting too late or too close and pulling up short for fear of the line. [web: https://volleyball.ca/uploads/Development/Coach/Skills/One_page_desc./DEV/English/Setting_%26_Attacking_Basics_Back_Row_for_e_module_skill_description.pdf ; https://www.sportplan.net/drills/Volleyball/trends/back-row-attack-offensive-threat-2025-04.jsp]
- Pipe = from position 6 (middle back), set between the middle and outside, usually fast and lowish; D (or "bic", "red") = from position 1 (right back) [memory]. A receiving outside hitter's back-row attack is usually the pipe: pass, then run the pipe in the same rally, the modern OH's job [memory].
- Back-row contact is a broad jump plus vertical: contact is about 1 to 1.5 m behind where a front-row contact would be, so the hit is deeper, and the angle into the block is flatter [estimate]. Teammates' landing space and the line judge: real details.
- **Transition footwork** after passing: 2 to 3 quick steps back out to the 3 m line plus a few meters while the setter is under the ball; most hitters are late because they watch their own pass [memory]. The eye cannot help with this (it reads hitters and setters, never ball destination); the map can (he knows where he must be).
- Tape on the floor: 1 tape mark 1 m behind the 3 m line for the penultimate, as Volleyball Canada describes. Countable: "pipes, 10 balls: line touched 2 times week 2, 0 times week 6".

## Part 8. Out-of-system high ball
- Coaching consensus: get wide and wait longer before starting the approach; keep the slow-to-fast rhythm; high balls are higher and slower so the whole timing resets; set up about 15 to 17 feet (4.5 to 5 m) off the net (1 coach's rule) without going so deep that a tight ball beats you; keep the ball in front. Dissent: Mark Lebedew argues high balls are overused and faster sets from more situations can be better. "Out of system" means different things to different coaches. [web: https://coachingvb.com/working-on-out-of-system-play/ ; https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/where-do-college-coaches-want-to-see-their-outside-hitters-start-from/ ; https://marklebedew.com/2019/01/07/thinking-about-high-balls/ ; https://volleycountry.com/training/7-tips-how-to-improve-your-spike-timing-of-high-sets]
- Options on the bad ball [memory]: hit high hands and off the outside blocker's fingers (the tool: Sho did it to Kuroo and Bokuto); deep corner over the block; roll shot to the hole behind the block; hit it "smart" into position 1 or 5 to keep it in play. An ace's out-of-system goal is a high kill rate with a very low error rate (keep the rally alive) rather than a kill every time. Real kill rates on out-of-system swings are much lower than in-system, roughly a 3rd in good men's play [memory].
- Countable drill [estimate]: Ukai tosses 10 high balls from the back corner, 2 blockers up (Tsukishima and a 3rd year, or a block board). Week 1: 3 kills, 3 errors, 4 in play. Week 6: 4 kills, 2 errors. Week 12: 5 kills, 1 error. 5 out of 10 against a real 2-man block in practice is a strong high school ace.
- What Sho asks Kageyama for [memory, canon-consistent]: "Higher. A ball off the net. I need the time." Kageyama asks hitters what they want (canon); this is a request, never a note on the quick.

## Part 9. Growth, nutrition, sleep

### 9.1 Growth plates and growth spurts
- PHV in boys around 13.5 to 14 [memory]; accelerated growth phase roughly 10.7 to 15.2 in academy soccer players; injury risk (overuse and growth-related) is highest around PHV; load spikes plus poor recovery raise illness and non-contact injury risk; landing and coordination work plus load adjustment may reduce injury in high-risk growers. [web: https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/can-we-reduce-injury-risk-during-the-adolescent-growth-spurt-an-i/ ; https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/maturity-associated-considerations-for-training-load-injury-risk-/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8343060]
- Growth plates in boys close mostly between about 15 and 19 [memory]; Sho's are still open, which is why the knee pain must be taken seriously and why the program avoids max lifts (no 1-rep max tests; he tests 5-rep sets) [memory, standard youth practice].
- Height detail: people are about 1 to 1.5 cm taller in the morning than at night (spinal discs compress during the day) [memory]. Dad's door-frame pencil and the reach chalk must be done at the same time of day, or the numbers lie.
- Sho's rate [estimate]: 183 in August; 183.5 in October; 184 by late November. Dad's pencil: *184. Nov.*

### 9.2 Nutrition
- Protein: adolescent athletes 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day; Sports Dietitians Australia 1.3 to 1.8 g/kg; a nitrogen-balance study of 14-year-old male soccer players found a recommended 1.4 g/kg/day; no extra benefit past about 3 g/kg. Energy needs: no simple formula; use growth and health markers (weight, growth, energy) to judge. [web: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17273884/ ; https://www.sportsdietitians.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Adolescent-Nutrition.pdf]
- For Sho at 68 kg: 90 to 120 g of protein a day [estimate]. In a Japanese home: rice with every meal, 2 eggs at breakfast, natto, grilled fish, tofu in miso soup, milk, chicken, onigiri with salmon after practice, a yogurt or cheese at night [memory]. Teen boys in daily practice plus lifting often need 3000 to 3500+ kcal [memory/estimate]. Mom's "You're thinner" is a real clinical signal (low energy availability) and her fix (more rice, an extra onigiri in the bag) is the real treatment.
- Timing [memory]: a snack with protein and carbs within an hour or so after training; the 7 a.m. lift means breakfast before (a banana and milk at 6:30) and a real breakfast after.

### 9.3 Sleep
- Milewski et al. 2014 (J Pediatr Orthop): adolescent athletes sleeping under 8 hours a night were 1.7 times more likely to be injured (another analysis: adjusted RR 2.0). Sleep and school grade were the strongest predictors; practice hours and strength training were not. Self-reported data; association only. [web: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6446394 ; https://cm.healio.com/news/orthopedics/20121025/milewski_10_3928_1081_597x_20121001_23_909304]
- Teens need 8 to 10 hours (AASM) [memory].
- **Soul use (never explained):** Satoshi died at 3:14 a.m. rewinding tape; arc 3 shows Sho at his desk at 4:15 a.m. The ace program's real "1 more" rule at night is the opposite: lights out by 10:30 for a 6:30 alarm (8 hours). The analyst brain fights it. The science gives him the reason in his own notebook: *< 8 h = 1.7x.* Mio enforces it.

## Part 10. Measurement: how a family would do it
- Standard wall method: stand side-on, feet flat, reach up with the near hand, mark the fingertips (standing reach); then jump and touch as high as possible with chalked fingers (jump reach); score is the difference; best of 3, rest between. A brick or block wall with horizontal lines helps; tape measure and chalk are the kit. [web: https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/vertjump.htm ; https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/standing-reach.htm ; https://www.volleyballalberta.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Volleyball-Alberta-Physical-Testing-Protocols-Manual-October-2025.pdf]
- **Japanese garage problem** [estimate]: inside a Japanese residential garage the ceiling is about 2.2 to 2.5 m; the reach mark must go on the **outside** wall (the block wall along the driveway or the house's 2-storey side wall). 318 cm means Dad on a stepladder with the tape and Mio on a stool with the chalk.
- What the garage wall can measure safely: standing reach, a standing 2-foot jump touch, a 1 to 2 step jump touch side-on to the wall. A full 4-step approach into a wall is a collision risk, so the **real spike reach is measured in the gym** (tape measure taped vertically on the gym wall or the basketball backboard support; or a ball hung on a string from the basketball hoop at a known height; the rim is 305 cm) [memory]. Plan: the garage wall gives the family numbers (standing 242, standing jump about 300); the gym gives the 318.
- Real noise [memory/estimate]: plus or minus 1 to 2 cm day to day; morning vs evening; shoes vs bare feet (about 2 to 3 cm); chalk on the middle finger vs the side of the hand; fatigue. Sho's rules: same day (Monday), same time, same shoes, best of 3, after the same warm-up. Mio writes them at the top of the *REACH* page.

## Part 13. Flags for the planner
- **325 by the prelims:** plausible, optimistic; the curve must flatten and dip (knee week, tired weeks). Recommend 323 to 324 on prelim days and 325 as a best-ever fresh measurement in the final's week.
- **Net height:** confirm whether Japanese high school boys play at 240 or 243 cm before writing scene 2 [memory says 240].
